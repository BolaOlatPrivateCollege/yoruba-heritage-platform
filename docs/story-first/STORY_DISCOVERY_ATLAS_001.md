# Story Discovery Atlas 001 — folklore, living memories, culture and learner engagement

**23 November 2026 · Working editorial source catalogue · Not publication approval**

## Objective

Build a **story-first Yorùbá cultural destination**, not a front-end for bibliography. An engaging account can begin with a disputed family saying, a question about an òrìṣà, a travelling trickster, an oral tradition, the memory of a market woman, a royal legend, a storyteller's riddle, a mystery around a place, or a myth that people tell in several ways. All are potentially welcome **when clearly typed and attributed**. The site should leave readers thinking “I heard this differently—may I tell you?” without compelling them to affirm or reject a sacred belief.

This is a first verified **internet discovery pass**, not a claim to have searched the entire internet or verified an entire book, oral history or collection. Add future sources through a rights and provenance queue. The original 113 article / 128 source scholarly archive and five recorded English documentary narrations stay unchanged.

## What we found worth pursuing

**Folktale, riddle, proverb and storytelling collections**
1. **Yoruba Folktales Revisited**, https://yorubatales.com/tales/ — 36 illustrated / voiced tale pages: Ìjàpá, animals, people, humour, motives and consequences. Site advertises **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**; verify all relevant asset licences, conditions, attribution, and the project's use before reuse. Prefer author collaboration or independent permissioned new tellings; **do not ingest or duplicate whole texts/media**.
2. **Yoruba Folktales Revisited — about**, https://yorubatales.com/about/ — a modern writer's discussion of why/how tortoise tales and narrative structure; useful creator partnership lead, not a fieldwork transcript.
3. **Yoruba Folktales Revisited — bibliography**, https://yorubatales.com/bibliography/ — discovery trail for older and Nigerian collections; authorship, edition and actual first-hand provenance require individual examination.
4. **National Library of Nigeria, *Tales of Yoruba Gods and Heroes***, https://nigeriareposit.nln.gov.ng/items/8362d8f8-e372-42b7-babc-9d2723373889 — bibliographic table of contents for Harold Courlander's **1973 copyrighted collection**. Topic seeds include Èṣù, Ọ̀ṣun, Ọbàtálá, Mọremí, Òrànmíyàn, Ṣàngó, Ọya, the Èṣìẹ̀ figures, twins and hunters. **Existence of a story title does not license use of that author's expression or validate an individual telling**.
5. **African Storybook** https://www.africanstorybook.org/about.php — free picture stories and Yorùbá translations; **check per-work and illustration/translation licence**. Some published books explicitly use CC BY 4.0 (example https://www.africanstorybook.org/read/downloadbooklet.php?a=1&d=0&id=47276&layout=booklet ); they aren't necessarily traditional Yorùbá stories and must not be mislabelled as such. This resource skews younger than secondary school, useful for accessibility / multilingual experiments.
6. **Storybooks Nigeria**, https://global-asp.github.io/storybooks-nigeria/about/ — African Storybook adaptations/read-along content in Nigerian languages and Pidgin; explore audio/text alignment and per-asset rights, not automatic copying.
7. **Bàbá O Ní Gbà — 20 Yorùbá riddles**, https://www.babaonigba.com/20-yoruba-riddles-and-their-surprising-meaning/ — contemporary Yoruba-language riddle examples and discussion of Àlọ́ Àpamọ̀/Àlọ́ Àpagbe. Need author and oral origins, dialect/orthography, permission to reproduce any wording.
8. **National Open University of Nigeria: African Communication Systems II**, https://nou.edu.ng/coursewarecontent/MAC116.pdf — explains short riddles and longer participatory tales. **Background only**; no copying assessed exercises or verses.
9. **Resounding Yorùbá narratives (2026)**, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21681392.2026.2708044 — scholarship on archived Àlọ́ performances, oral pacing, silence, song and call-and-response. **Research off-stage; not licensed source audio or an instruction to imitate a community ceremony literally**.

**Community voices and first-person cultural memory**
10. **Digital Nollywood / Yorùbá Veterans Oral History Project**, https://digitalnollywood.ku.edu/ — actual interviews with veterans of Yorùbá filmmaking, collected by Rasaq Malik and collaborators. **Contact for speaker/recording licences and transcription permissions**; prospective first-hand “I was there” stories, do not copy interviews.
11. **Royal History Museum of Oyo State — Oral Traditions**, https://royalhistorymuseum.org/oral-history — community-based oríkì, family histories, proverbs and memories as partnership leads. Its institutional summaries do **not** authenticate any imagined local elder's particular story. See also https://www.royalhistorymuseum.org/oriki and https://www.royalhistorymuseum.org/olubadan-ibadan .
12. **Royal History Museum — Our Work**, https://www.royalhistorymuseum.org/our-work — potential locally supervised, rights-aware collaboration (contact organisers; do not state partnership already exists).
13. **Breathe Nigeria** https://breathenigeria.com.ng/index.php — community heritage contributions reviewed before publishing, with folktales/memories as categories. Its intake style is a **benchmark**, not access to its submissions.
14. **University of Ibadan journal/academic material and local ethnography**, e.g. Igbomina Èṣìẹ̀ research https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/soapstone-sculptures-esie — use only to identify community-specific questions and possible speakers; don't make the learner's version a museum report.
15. **Regional community heritage practice — UNESCO Gelede**, https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/oral-heritage-of-gelede-00002 and https://ich.unesco.org/en/projects/action-plan-for-the-safeguarding-and-promotion-of-the-oral-tradition-of-gelede-00006 — community context across Benin, Nigeria, Togo and living-performance transmission; consult specific practitioners before treating an authored reconstruction as an authentic ceremony.
16. **UNESCO, Ifá divination system**, https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/ifa-divination-system-00146 — living tradition, oral corpus and custodianship; **no fabricated ọ̀dù, verse, prediction or secret ritual**. Sacred narratives and beliefs may be presented with permission without requiring “laboratory proof.”

**Sacred names, contested meanings and lively misconceptions**
17. **Olódùmarè**: site-owned `SACRED_NAMES_RESEARCH_NOTE.md` and `names-etymology.js` already distinguish the recognised religious referent from contested proposed word divisions; use as **backstage checks** while collecting proper first-person sacred-name explanations. Research leads David T. Adamo https://scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S2305-08532019000100026&script=sci_arttext and Bamgbose bibliographic record https://www.africabib.org/rec.php?RID=191712140 .
18. **Èṣù and the Devil translation controversy**: site-owned `SACRED_WORLD_RESEARCH_GUIDE.md`, plus Sandra Nickel https://www.latl.leeds.ac.uk/article/spreading-which-word-philological-theological-and-socio-political-considerations-behind-the-nineteenth-century-bible-translation-into-yoruba/ ; practitioner commentary https://www.foluoyefeso.com/post/esu-the-misunderstood-orisha-reclaiming-yoruba-spirituality-from-colonial-misconceptions . Keep religious voices distinct, and do not assert one unquestioned etymology or caricature any faith. See Task #35.
19. **Èṣìẹ̀, people turned to stone?** local petrification narratives alongside the known carved figures; invite actual community tellers about what the story has meant. Scholarship off-stage https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/soapstone-sculptures-esie ; **do not present physical transformation as archaeologically verified**.
20. **Oríkì and sayings as living heritage**, https://www.royalhistorymuseum.org/oriki ; narratives of names, town identity, family praise and varied meanings. Seek permission for reproducing original text, translation and recorded performance.
21. **Nigerian Federal Ministry cultural heritage inventories**, https://fmacce.gov.ng/intangible-cultural-heritage — heritage practices and regional leads; not a licence to reproduce community ceremonies.
22. **UNESCO ethics for intangible cultural heritage**, https://ich.unesco.org/en/ethics-and-ich-00866 — living tradition custodians need consent, dialogue and participation. Rights, privacy and sacred boundaries remain meaningful even when an image or oral account is available online.

**Not yet confirmed / prospective future source leads:** TikTok, Facebook, YouTube commentary on Èṣù and origin myths shared by the owner, unknown genealogy accounts, anonymous “elder said” social posts. Without actual audio, known speaker and permission **do not attribute or quote video content**. Contact culturally grounded projects before using supposedly “authentic” testimonies.

## Prioritisation: first 12 fully written chapters

**Four initial content lanes** at 3 stories each:
- **Suspense/legend:** Èṣù hat; Ìjàpá shell; Èṣìẹ̀ stones.
- **Everyday/craft:** Àdìrẹ maker; market woman's memory; the proverb that changed a quarrel.
- **Living belief:** Olódùmarè name; Ọ̀ṣun and the river; alternate Ilé-Ifẹ̀ beginnings.
- **First-hand/community:** theatre veteran; place-name account; town history told by families.

Do not publish a lead as an “authentic community account” until an actual named-permissioned teller/source exists. Create **original fiction clearly labelled** where no permissioned traditional version is available; those creative tales may inspire people to offer the real versions they know.

## Minimal story experience and engagement loop

1. Hero question, compelling scene/character, 3–5 short chapters or ~900–1,800 words; younger-reader variant if needed. No footnote/bibliography inserted between the story's scenes.
2. Soft story-type and storyteller/community credit: **Traditional tale — community-specific telling**, **Local legend — shared by [consenting source]**, **First-hand memory**, **Original fiction**, **Disputed account**, or **Documented historical story**.
3. Optional matching narrated audio and **AI-generated illustration** (only if the exact story and rights are cleared); accessible text remains primary.
4. End card: **Do you know another version?** / **What did people tell you as a child?** / **Correct this account** / **Share a voice note** (voice notes only after consent/privacy/security service exists). One imaginative classroom question.
5. Moderator can add attributed variant **beside**, not overwrite, earlier stories; show version history, withdrawal and provenance. Don't use popularity to declare community belief true or false.
6. “Scholar's Archive” is a quiet separate path for documentary articles/footnotes, not an essay attached to every tale.

## Rights decisions before generation or publishing

- **No wholesale harvesting of online story texts or media.** Use sources to discover folklore plots and real collaborators, then obtain independent authorised retellings or write clearly original fiction.
- Published oral history requires informed speaker/storyteller consent (including correct story context and credit/anonymous options), accurate representation and review/withdrawal pathway.
- Images must be original, commissioned or generated under reviewed terms, with labels: `AI-generated illustration of a fictional scene — not an archival photograph or documented depiction of a historical event/ritual`. Sensitive real sacred figures/performances require additional custodian review even if the illustration is imaginary.
- Do not copy names, accounts or privacy-sensitive events of living people into rumours that might imply wrongdoing. For minors use supervised/school/guardian channel and no public personal contact details.
- Keep full research provenance *in the separate editorial/scholar space*; reader-facing pages stay rich and natural.
