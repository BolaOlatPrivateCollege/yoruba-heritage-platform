# Ìjàpá and the Feast Nobody Could Finish

*Original imaginative fiction featuring Ìjàpá, the tortoise associated with cleverness and mischief in many Yorùbá storytelling traditions. This exact plot, dialogue and cast are invented; it is not a transcript of a named teller's àlọ́ or a claim about one community's authentic version.*

## Chapter 1 — A smell too wonderful to ignore

Ìjàpá knew three things about the morning. The sun had risen, his stomach was empty, and somewhere beyond the trees somebody was cooking something extraordinary.

He followed the smell past a patch of cassava, around a tree with roots like bent knees, and into a clearing where the animals were preparing a feast. There were baskets of roasted yam, bean cakes, stewed vegetables and fresh fruit. Birds were tying bright ribbons between branches. The old goat, who liked to organise anything that could be organised, stood beside a clay pot and read from a list.

"One dish from every household," Goat announced. "One place for every guest. And nobody eats until everyone arrives."

Ìjàpá smiled. It was a magnificent rule, he thought, because rules were doors if you knew how to find the hinges.

He had brought nothing. His garden needed watering, and the vegetables there were too small to fill even his own bowl. He looked at the heap of food and then at the list in Goat's hoof.

"I have brought a special contribution," he said.

Goat glanced up. "Where is it?"

"A promise," said Ìjàpá. "I can make this feast remembered for generations."

The birds stopped tying ribbons. A young squirrel asked whether promises could be eaten. Ìjàpá pretended not to hear.

"I propose a contest," he continued. "Whoever tells the grandest story about the food may have the first taste."

Goat frowned. "That was not our agreement."

"Then perhaps," Ìjàpá replied, making his voice mournful, "you do not want the feast to be memorable."

The guests looked at one another. Everyone wanted a celebration worth remembering. By the time Goat opened his mouth to object, the first contestant was already standing.

## Chapter 2 — The contest changes shape

Parrot described yams so tender that even the wind would stop to admire them. Hare claimed his mother's bean cakes once persuaded a raincloud to wait until after supper. Nobody believed Hare, but they laughed, and laughter made the food look even more delicious.

When Ìjàpá's turn arrived, he climbed onto a stump and lowered his voice.

"In a distant clearing," he began, "there is a pot that never empties. The more a guest takes, the fuller it becomes. I have seen it myself."

"Where?" asked Squirrel.

"Beyond the third hill."

"Can we visit?"

"Only after tasting the food in this clearing," said Ìjàpá. "The pot chooses guests who know a feast when they see one."

Goat tapped his hoof against the ground. "You are inventing conditions for a pot we cannot see."

"Are you certain it does not exist?" Ìjàpá asked.

Goat hesitated. The guests murmured. Nobody wanted to be the one who refused a magical invitation.

Ìjàpá saw his chance. "As the storyteller of the greatest feast, I shall test the first bowl. To make sure the meal is worthy of our journey."

He reached for a bean cake.

Goat blocked him with a wooden spoon. "We agreed to share. You proposed a contest, but you did not earn the right to rewrite every other promise."

Ìjàpá bowed politely and stepped back. Yet he had noticed something: when everyone imagined an endless pot, they began guarding their own bowls less carefully.

There were still two hours before the feast officially began. Ìjàpá decided two hours was a great deal of time for a clever creature.

## Chapter 3 — One name, many invitations

Ìjàpá found a leaf and scratched a message upon it: *The feast committee asks that you prepare a little extra for the guest of honour.* He carried the leaf to Hare's house.

"Who is the guest of honour?" Hare asked.

"That is a surprise," said Ìjàpá.

He took the same message to Parrot and Squirrel. By noon, every household had prepared an extra plate. Each believed the honour belonged to someone important. Each hoped the extra plate would make a good impression.

Ìjàpá returned to the clearing with a broad smile.

"What wonderful generosity," he told Goat. "I shall personally help carry the guest's food."

"Which guest?"

"One who has not yet arrived."

At the edge of the feast, behind the tall grass, he arranged the extra plates in a neat row. One taste became two; two became a third. Soon his stomach felt like a basket packed too tightly.

A small voice spoke behind him.

"Why does the guest of honour look exactly like you?"

It was Squirrel, whose family had sent the extra roasted yam. Her tail stood straight with surprise.

Ìjàpá tried to hide the plates beneath a large leaf. The leaf was much too small.

"I am protecting them," he said.

"By eating them?"

"By making sure nobody else eats them."

Squirrel stared at him for so long that he began to wish she would simply shout.

Instead she asked, "If you can invite a guest who does not exist, can you also make food that does not exist?"

Ìjàpá had no answer. That was unusual enough to worry him.

## Chapter 4 — The feast without a beginning

Squirrel did not announce what she had seen. She went quietly to Goat, and Goat asked the families what they had been told. Three different messengers, the same leaf, and no named guest.

The birds untied the ribbons near the serving table. They had worked all morning, and now they wanted to know whether anything about the feast had been true.

"The food is real," Goat told them. "So is the work people did to make it."

"But the endless pot?" asked Parrot.

"Nobody here has seen it."

Hare looked at Ìjàpá, who was trying to appear smaller than his shell. "We were ready to climb three hills because you spoke as if you had already travelled there."

Ìjàpá lifted his head. "It might exist somewhere."

"Then say *might*," said Squirrel. "Do not say *I saw* if you did not."

The animals gathered around the serving table, but no one reached for a plate. The extra food had been eaten. Goat worried there would not be enough for the youngest guests. Parrot was angry that her contribution had been taken. Hare accused Squirrel of spying. The sun moved higher, and the feast that was meant to begin at noon still had no beginning.

Ìjàpá looked at the bowls and understood that hunger had not been the only thing growing. Suspicion had spread from plate to plate. Every guest now wondered whether the others had hidden something.

He knew how to invent an excuse. He knew how to turn an accusation into a joke. For once neither talent seemed useful.

"Tell us," Goat said, "what happened to the guest of honour?"

Ìjàpá looked at the ground. "I made him up," he said. "And then I ate his supper."

## Chapter 5 — A difficult kind of cleverness

Goat did not ask the gathering to forget what happened. He asked them to count the food that remained.

The children would eat first. Each adult would take a smaller portion. The musicians and the guests who had travelled far would be served before anyone went back for seconds. When everyone understood the plan, the feast finally began.

Ìjàpá sat apart beneath a tree. It was a strange punishment: nobody had tied him up, yet he could not bring himself to join the circle.

After a while Squirrel brought him a small piece of roasted yam.

"I thought you would be pleased if I came to argue," she said.

"I would," he admitted. "An argument is easier than this."

"Then what will you do?"

He considered claiming that the entire deception had been a test of their generosity. That sounded clever. He could imagine the speech already, with its pauses and grand ending.

But it would be another story told to escape the truth.

"I will bring back what I took," he said.

"How?"

"My garden."

"Your vegetables are still small."

"Then I will work until they are ready. Perhaps I can bring them to the next gathering."

Squirrel nodded, but did not call the matter settled. "And the story about the endless pot?"

"I shall tell it again," said Ìjàpá. Her expression darkened. He quickly added, "But this time I shall begin, 'Here is something I imagined.'"

For the first time all day, Squirrel laughed.

## Chapter 6 — What the shell remembered

Weeks later the clearing filled with guests again. Ìjàpá arrived pushing a basket of vegetables so full that one leaf dragged along the ground. He had watered his garden through hot afternoons and chased goats away from the seedlings. His back ached. His shell carried scratches from the path.

Goat examined the basket. "A contribution," he said. "Not a promise?"

"A contribution," Ìjàpá replied.

Parrot inspected the leaves. Hare counted the portions. Squirrel made certain that the children had plates.

When the dishes had been shared, somebody called for a story. Every face turned towards Ìjàpá.

He climbed onto the old stump and surveyed the clearing. He could have spoken of an invisible palace, a magical king or a pot beyond the hills. Instead he told a story about a tortoise so hungry that he tried to become the guest of honour at a feast to which everyone had already been invited.

The animals laughed at the ridiculous disguise. They laughed at the leaf too small to hide the plates. Some laughed softly when he reached the part where his invented guest had eaten all the extra food.

Then Ìjàpá said, "The tortoise was me."

Nobody laughed for a moment.

"The endless pot was not something I saw," he continued. "It was something I wanted you to believe. I made it sound true because I wanted what was in your bowls."

Goat watched him closely. "Why tell us that?"

"Because the story is better when you know what sort of story it is."

Parrot gave one thoughtful squawk. Squirrel raised her bowl. The clearing filled again with conversation, argument, laughter and the sound of people eating together.

In later years, some told the tale with a magical pot, others with an imaginary prince, and others with a hungry tortoise who claimed to be two guests at once. No one at that fictional feast could have predicted every version. But the children liked to ask one question before the storyteller began:

"Did Ìjàpá really see the pot?"

And the storyteller, if paying attention, answered, "Come closer. First I must tell you what I know—and what I am making up."
