# Olókun: The Secrets Beneath the Water

*Original literary fiction inspired by Yorùbá traditions associating Olókun with the depths of water, wealth and mystery. The settlement, characters, discoveries and dialogue are invented. This is not a transmitted sacred narrative, an archaeological report or a historical reconstruction.*

## Chapter 1 — The visitor who promised gold

The stranger arrived with a small brass box and a story that was too wonderful for anyone to ignore.

Somewhere beneath the water, he claimed, lay the possessions of a merchant whose boat had disappeared long before the oldest person in the settlement was born. He had a drawing that showed a curved shoreline, a tree and three marks beside a dark patch of water.

"If you help me find it," he told the people gathered at the landing, "none of you will need to work again."

Fẹ́mi, whose father repaired fishing nets, felt his heart leap. He imagined a new roof for their house, a fine boat for his father and enough money to buy books without asking anybody's permission.

His friend Dáramọ́lá studied the drawing.

"Who made this?"

The stranger closed the brass box. "A man who knew things."

"Did he see the boat sink?"

"Perhaps."

"Where is he now?"

The stranger laughed. "You ask a great many questions for somebody who has never been wealthy."

By sunset, the story had reached every household. With each telling, the lost merchant grew richer and the drawing grew older. Someone claimed the box itself had been recovered from the depths. Nobody could remember who had first said so.

At home, Fẹ́mi told his father that their troubles might soon be over.

His father held up a torn net.

"Before you believe a man who promises that nobody will ever work again, ask him why he needs other people to do his work."

Fẹ́mi thought this was a disappointingly ordinary answer to an extraordinary discovery.

## Chapter 2 — The keeper of the old things

Dáramọ́lá persuaded Fẹ́mi to visit Ìyá Àbẹ́ní, a retired trader who kept a collection of family objects in a room overlooking the lagoon. There were woven baskets, old photographs, decorated vessels and account books whose pages had turned brown.

Fẹ́mi expected her to recognise the stranger's drawing immediately.

Instead, she asked them to describe everything they had heard, separating what they had actually seen from what other people had told them.

When Fẹ́mi finished, she placed a wooden bowl on the table.

"My mother remembered Olókun when she spoke of the deep waters and the riches people cannot always see," she said. "Her understanding belonged to her family and community. Other traditions remember Olókun in different ways."

Fẹ́mi asked whether this meant treasures could be hidden beneath the water.

"It means that we should not mistake our desire for knowledge."

She opened an account book. One page recorded the cost of repairing a boat. Another contained the names of traders who had contributed to a neighbour's recovery after a loss.

"Is this treasure?" she asked.

Fẹ́mi looked at the faded handwriting. "Not the kind we are looking for."

"Perhaps not. But without these pages, I would know much less about the people who made my life possible."

Dáramọ́lá asked whether they might copy the names for a school project. Ìyá Àbẹ́ní explained that some entries were private and others could be shared with permission.

The children left with no clue about the merchant's possessions. They did, however, carry a new question: how could they recognise the value of something before they understood what it was?

## Chapter 3 — A secret with too many owners

The stranger's plan changed as more people gathered around him.

He now wanted a contribution from each household. The money, he said, would pay for equipment and skilled assistance. Anyone who refused would receive no share of the discovery.

Fẹ́mi had saved a little money from helping his father. He took the coins from a cloth pouch and counted them twice.

Dáramọ́lá asked whether the stranger had permission to investigate the site. Fẹ́mi became impatient.

"If we wait until everything is certain, somebody else will find it."

"What if there is nothing there?"

He did not answer.

At the landing, the stranger was showing his drawing to several older boat workers. One recognised the tree but explained that it had stood in a different position before a storm many years earlier. Another said the shape of the shoreline had changed.

The stranger dismissed their objections. He had a secret, he declared, that ordinary people could not understand.

Fẹ́mi noticed that the man never explained how he knew the merchant had existed. He spoke instead about the wonderful things each listener might buy.

A woman who had contributed money asked for a written record. The stranger promised to provide one later.

That evening, Fẹ́mi returned home with his coins still in their pouch. He was not yet convinced that the treasure was imaginary. He was beginning to realise that the stranger's certainty was not evidence.

He wondered how many other people had noticed the same thing but were afraid to speak because they wanted the promise to be true.

## Chapter 4 — What the waves delivered

Two mornings later, after rough weather had passed, a boat worker named Tádé noticed an unfamiliar object lying among the debris on the shore.

People gathered at a safe distance. The object appeared to be part of a decorated ceramic vessel, worn by water and time. Nobody knew its age or where it had come from.

The stranger hurried forward.

"There!" he cried. "The merchant's treasure!"

Dáramọ́lá asked how he could know that from a single object.

He ignored her and announced that the discovery proved his drawing was genuine.

Tádé contacted the appropriate local authorities and asked for advice on the find. The object was left undisturbed while qualified people were consulted. The children were told not to search dangerous areas or disturb anything else.

Fẹ́mi watched as the stranger's excitement turned to anger.

"Why should other people decide what belongs to us?" the man demanded.

Ìyá Àbẹ́ní, who had arrived with Fẹ́mi's father, answered calmly.

"We do not even know what it is. How can we already know who owns it?"

Later, a heritage specialist explained that a fragment might be recent or old, locally made or carried from elsewhere. Its context mattered. A story attached to an object without evidence could obscure its real significance.

Fẹ́mi had imagined a revelation that would end every argument. Instead, the discovery had produced more questions.

The stranger left the gathering before the specialist finished speaking.

For the first time, Fẹ́mi felt excited about the questions themselves.

## Chapter 5 — The wealth that could not be divided

The community arranged a meeting to discuss the stranger's promises and the money people had contributed. Several households had given him more than they could comfortably afford.

The elders agreed to seek appropriate help with the disputed payments and to document what had occurred. They did not accuse everyone who had believed him of foolishness.

Fẹ́mi listened to a trader describe the debts she hoped the promised treasure would settle. A boat worker had wanted better equipment. An elderly man had hoped to help his grandchildren.

Their hopes were real, even if the stranger's claims were unproven.

At a second gathering, Ìyá Àbẹ́ní brought some of the objects she had permission to share. She told the children how families had supported one another through difficult seasons. Other residents contributed recollections, carefully distinguishing personal memories from accounts heard from earlier generations.

A local craftworker demonstrated how a damaged basket could be repaired. Boat workers spoke about changes they had observed in the waters. Traders described skills they had learned from their parents.

Fẹ́mi and Dáramọ́lá helped prepare a small exhibition with their teacher. Every object had a label identifying its source, its known history and the questions that remained unanswered.

The mysterious ceramic fragment was not displayed as treasure. Its origin was still being assessed.

At the end of the gathering, Fẹ́mi asked Ìyá Àbẹ́ní whether all these things could really be considered wealth.

She pointed to the people exchanging knowledge around the room.

"If two people share an account and both remember it afterwards, which one has become poorer?"

He smiled. He could not divide that kind of wealth into coins.

## Chapter 6 — A question for the deep

Months later, Fẹ́mi sat at the landing with a notebook balanced on his knees. His father was repairing another net, and Dáramọ́lá was trying to draw the changing patterns of light on the water.

The promised fortune had never been established. The old drawing was not proof of a lost merchant, and the fragment had not revealed the extraordinary history the stranger claimed for it.

Fẹ́mi still wanted to know what lay beneath the water.

He had learned, however, that curiosity did not require him to possess everything he encountered. Some questions belonged to specialists. Some objects required protection. Some traditions could only be shared by the people entrusted with them.

When Ìyá Àbẹ́ní came to the landing, he asked her to tell him again how her mother remembered Olókun.

She sat beside the children and described the family understanding she had inherited. She made clear that neighbouring communities and other traditions might speak differently.

Fẹ́mi recorded nothing until she gave permission. Afterwards, he read back what he had written and invited her to correct it.

"Will you put this in your exhibition?" she asked.

"Only the parts you want others to hear."

She nodded.

The three of them watched a boat moving across the distant water. Its reflection disappeared whenever the wind stirred the surface.

Fẹ́mi remembered the stranger who had promised to explain every mystery and divide every treasure. He no longer needed such a promise.

There were things he hoped to discover, things he would never know, and things he might learn only by listening to another person.

He closed his notebook as the evening light faded.

The depths kept their secrets. The boy kept his questions.
