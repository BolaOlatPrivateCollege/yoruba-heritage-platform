# Mọ́remí and the Fire in the Reeds

*An original dramatic retelling inspired by stories told of Mọ́remí Àjàṣorò in Ilé-Ifẹ̀. It follows one imagined arrangement of courage, disguise and costly promises. The scenes, private conversations and character of Adúnní are invented; this is not a transcription of a named custodian's account. Different tellings of Mọ́remí and the Ẹ̀dì tradition should be heard in their own voices.*

## Chapter 1 — The night nobody named

Nobody lit a lamp near the northern gate after dark.

Once, Adúnní remembered, the children had raced each other through that gate and returned with clay on their feet. Now its wooden doors shut before sunset, and the old sellers at the market hurried home whenever an unfamiliar sound moved through the trees.

"They come wrapped in things no spear can touch," said the potter.

"They are shadows wearing the forest," said the woman who sold salt.

No one agreed about what the raiders looked like. Everyone agreed about the fear left behind.

Mọ́remí listened beside the market well. She stood without jewellery, carrying a plain water pot. People spoke more freely when they believed nobody important was listening.

"What did you see?" she asked the potter.

"I saw them run through the burning grass without burning," he answered.

"And before the fire?"

He hesitated. "I heard leaves rattling. Even though there was no wind."

That evening Mọ́remí walked to the gate with Adúnní, a young attendant who had once followed her everywhere and now feared to follow anywhere.

"You cannot fight a shadow," Adúnní whispered.

"Perhaps not," Mọ́remí said. "But shadows are made by something."

The watchman warned them to leave. Beyond the gate a bird cried once, and then the forest went quiet.

Mọ́remí placed her hand on the old wood. The city did not need another brave speech, she thought. It needed someone willing to discover what everybody else had been too frightened to ask.

## Chapter 2 — A promise no one could hear

For three nights the palace argued.

One speaker demanded more guards. Another wanted a thicker wall. A third proposed shutting every road until the danger passed. Each offered an answer that left the mystery itself untouched.

Mọ́remí watched the speakers quarrel. Whenever she asked how the raiders disappeared into the trees, their voices grew louder, as if volume could cover ignorance.

At last she spoke.

"Suppose one of us went where they go."

Silence filled the hall.

"That would be a journey without a return," someone replied.

"Then tell me what else will keep the children safe."

Nobody answered. The palace could refuse her request, but it could not promise that waiting would end the attacks.

Later, alone beneath a darkening sky, Mọ́remí stood before a river. The promise she made seemed lighter when spoken than it would feel when remembered. She did not hear a voice rise from the water. She heard instead the sound of the current moving without regard for what humans wanted.

"If I return," she whispered, "let me return with an answer."

She made a promise whose meaning she believed she understood.

Adúnní found her before dawn. "They say you mean to let them take you."

"I mean to learn why our people cannot stop them."

"And if the price is greater than you imagine?"

Mọ́remí looked towards the sleeping city. "Then I will have to face the truth about that too."

She took off her finely woven wrapper, folded it carefully and put on the plain clothes of someone travelling alone.

## Chapter 3 — The people beneath the leaves

The raiders appeared at the edge of a field just as the sun went down.

From far away they seemed taller than people. Great shapes shuddered around them, covered in layers that hissed and rattled as they moved. Mọ́remí did not run. She let them find her, and when they led her through the forest she counted each turn of the path.

The journey stretched beyond midnight. A creek crossed the trail. Two fallen trees formed a bridge. Somewhere an owl called and was answered by a human voice.

At last they reached a clearing where fires burned among ordinary cooking pots.

Mọ́remí stopped so suddenly that her captors stumbled.

The terrifying figures removed their coverings. Beneath them were men who looked tired and hungry. One warmed his hands. Another searched for a missing sandal. A boy no older than Adúnní complained that his shoulders ached under the weight of the disguise.

The creatures of the dark had feet that blistered.

Mọ́remí forced herself not to stare.

She learned their habits without showing how closely she watched. The coverings were made from dry plant fibres and leaves, woven into shapes that changed a person's outline. They made an unsettling sound when moved rapidly. The people who wore them relied on fear as much as weapons.

In the clearing, nobody treated Mọ́remí as a heroine. She was a captive who had to find ways of being useful, of remembering voices, and of waiting for the one moment when a guarded path might lie open.

The first week she learned where water was drawn. The second she learned which watchman slept through the hottest hour. The third she learned that the path to the creek flooded whenever rain fell in the hills.

She thought of the old potter's words: *They ran through burning grass without burning.*

He had seen a trick from a distance. He had not imagined someone could survive close enough to explain it.

## Chapter 4 — The door left open

The chance arrived with a storm.

Rain battered the roofs and drove the guards beneath an overhang. A cooking fire went out. The creek rose against its banks, and the watchman responsible for the northern trail left to find a rope.

Mọ́remí slipped beyond the last hut.

Branches tore at her sleeves. Wet leaves slapped her face. Once she heard a shout behind her, and her legs became so heavy with fear that she thought she would fall. She crawled beneath a fallen trunk and remained still as footsteps passed.

When she reached the creek it was deeper than she remembered.

There was no bridge. The two trees had shifted. For a terrible moment she considered turning back.

Then she heard Adúnní's question again: *What if the price is greater than you imagine?*

Mọ́remí entered the water.

She reached the other bank with bleeding palms and found the path to Ilé-Ifẹ̀ just before daybreak. The city watch nearly refused to open the gate; they believed anyone approaching from the raiders' forest might be a trap.

"Look at me," she called. "Do I look like a shadow?"

Adúnní ran to the gate first. She recognised the voice. A moment later the bolts were pulled back.

Mọ́remí had scarcely crossed the threshold before the questions began. Where had she been? Who were the strangers? Were they spirits? How did they vanish?

She raised a hand.

"Not all that frightens us is beyond understanding. Their coverings are dry, woven and carried on ordinary shoulders."

Then she told the people what she had seen.

## Chapter 5 — When fear caught fire

Knowledge changed the city's defence.

Watchers learned to recognise the rustle of the coverings. The guards arranged for light at the gate and made plans to challenge the attackers' disguises when they returned. Those who had once fled from the first movement in the trees prepared to stand together.

Mọ́remí did not ask the soldiers to boast about killing strangers. She asked them to remember what terror had stolen from the city: sleep, trade, peace and trust.

When the next attack came, the same towering shapes approached under the moon.

The watchmen trembled. Even after hearing Mọ́remí's explanation, it was difficult not to see monsters where they had always expected monsters.

Then one of the shapes caught fire.

A dry covering flared and fell. A frightened man broke from it and fled. Another stumbled and dropped the leaves around his shoulders. The illusion that had held the city in fear began to fail.

From the walls, Adúnní watched as the raiders scattered. She wanted to cry out in triumph, but Mọ́remí stood beside her in silence.

"You were right," Adúnní said. "They were people."

"That is the hardest part to remember," Mọ́remí answered. "Even when we are afraid of what people have done."

By morning news had spread through the streets. Market stalls reopened. Children walked to the well without counting every leaf that moved.

The city wanted a celebration.

Mọ́remí wanted a moment alone.

## Chapter 6 — The part of the story that hurts

The river was unchanged.

It passed over stones with the same patient sound it had made when Mọ́remí first stood beside it. The city had gained its courage; her own heart had become heavier.

The vow remained.

Then came the demand that made every victory song feel bitter: the vow required Mọ́remí to surrender the child she loved. No treasure, no procession and no grateful words could ease the terror of hearing the price spoken aloud.

Adúnní found Mọ́remí kneeling beside the water.

"They are calling your name in the market," she said.

"I know."

"They say you saved us."

Mọ́remí looked at her. "What do they say it cost?"

Adúnní had heard rumours. She could not bear to repeat them.

"They do not know."

"Then they know only half the story."

At the next gathering, people came to praise her courage. Some wanted the tale to end where the city was saved, with the shadows defeated and the drums sounding. Others insisted the final part mattered most: the weight of a vow and a mother's sorrow.

Mọ́remí did not tell the assembled people that courage cancels grief. Nor did she tell them that sacrifice becomes easy when others are grateful.

"Remember the city," she said. "But remember what it cost."

Generations later, the name Mọ́remí would be spoken in different houses, at gatherings, and in commemorations associated with Ilé-Ifẹ̀. People would tell of her daring in their own ways. Some would speak first of disguise and intelligence. Some would speak of a mother's love. Some would speak of the Ẹ̀dì tradition and the many meanings carried by collective remembrance.

And there would always be somebody like Adúnní, listening from the edge of the gathering, wondering whether a hero was a person who never felt afraid—or a person who knew the cost and went anyway.

The river kept flowing. The story kept being told.
