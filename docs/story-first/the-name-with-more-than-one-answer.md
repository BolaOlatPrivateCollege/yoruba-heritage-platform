# The Name That Would Not Fit Into One Answer

*An original fictional mystery about language, belief, memory and the different meanings people carry.*

**For readers:** All the students, relatives, teachers and conversations are imaginary. The religious name Olódùmarè is widely used in Yorùbá traditions, but no fictional character in this story speaks as an authenticated elder or representative of every community. Suggested word-breakdowns are interpretations, not established historical etymologies.

## Chapter 1 — The question on the blackboard

Fẹ́mi was certain he would win the class challenge.

The question looked easier than last week's mathematics test and much easier than the science quiz that had ended with three people arguing about whether a tomato belonged with fruit.

On the blackboard, their teacher had written one word:

**Olódùmarè.**

Underneath it she added:

**What does this name mean? Who taught you?**

“That's two questions,” Fẹ́mi whispered to his friend Zárà.

“Perhaps the second is the difficult one,” she replied.

Their teacher, Mrs Adéyẹmí, told them to return with an explanation. Not just a word from a search result, she said, but the explanation they had encountered, where it came from, and why it mattered to the person who used it.

A student at the back raised his hand. “Can I bring three explanations if people disagree?”

Mrs Adéyẹmí smiled. “Bring as many as you can explain honestly.”

That was when the trouble began.

Fẹ́mi loved being first. He liked answers that could be memorised, written in a neat line and rewarded with a bright red tick. A question that might accept three answers sounded like a mistake in the examination paper.

He opened his notebook, drew a line down the page and wrote:

**THE ONE CORRECT MEANING.**

Zárà looked over his shoulder.

“That's a very brave heading,” she said.

## Chapter 2 — The answer everyone already knew

That afternoon Fẹ́mi asked the first person he met.

His older cousin was working near the front door, trying to persuade a stubborn phone charger to function.

“Olódùmarè means the Almighty,” the cousin replied without looking up.

“Thank you,” Fẹ́mi said. One answer. Task completed.

But then his aunt heard them.

“That is one way to explain how people use the name,” she said. “It doesn't settle every question about its parts.”

“What parts?”

She considered the word. “You may have heard people divide it into sounds and give each a meaning. I have heard explanations about greatness, power that remains, and a source that is never exhausted.”

“So which one is right?”

“I didn't say they were all the same claim.”

Fẹ́mi frowned. “I only need an answer for school.”

“Then write down whose answer it is.”

This was becoming inconvenient.

His cousin, now battling a second charger, interrupted. “If you ask three people, you will get four explanations. Just use the search engine.”

Fẹ́mi did.

In a few minutes he found neat lists. Some treated the name as though every syllable had a single, perfectly certain meaning. Others described an inexhaustible pot or source. A few pages warned that these breakdowns were debated.

The search results agreed on something important about religious usage: many Yorùbá accounts describe Olódùmarè as supreme creator or ultimate source.

But the origin of the name itself seemed to slip away each time somebody tried to pin it down.

Fẹ́mi stared at his heading.

**THE ONE CORRECT MEANING.**

He began to suspect it had been a mistake.

## Chapter 3 — The argument at the bookshop

On Saturday Zárà took him to a bookshop, where they met Kúnlé, a student from another school.

Kúnlé listened to the challenge and grinned.

“My uncle says the important part is that the source never runs dry,” he said. “He uses that explanation when telling stories about provision.”

Fẹ́mi opened his notebook. “But a website says the beginning sounds like a word for an owner or possessor. Then another part is about fullness. Then the end is about permanence.”

“Does your website say who first divided it that way?”

Fẹ́mi checked.

He had copied the neatest answer and forgotten to copy where it came from.

Kúnlé asked another question. “Could someone use a name in prayer even if they don't know exactly how it was formed hundreds of years ago?”

Zárà nodded. “My grandmother knows what my name means in our family. That doesn't mean she invented all the sounds in it.”

That was annoyingly sensible.

Fẹ́mi imagined people arguing over his own name. His family had chosen it for a reason. But another person might use the same name, explain it differently, or tell a story about somebody who once carried it. The explanation of why a family valued a name was not automatically the history of every syllable.

He shut the notebook.

For the first time, he was more interested in the people behind the explanations than in choosing the shortest one.

Kúnlé found a blank page and drew three boxes.

In the first he wrote **WHAT THE NAME REFERS TO**.

In the second: **WHAT PEOPLE SAY IT MEANS**.

In the third: **HOW THE WORD WAS FORMED**.

“Are you telling me these questions can have different answers?”

“I am telling you,” Kúnlé replied, “that your teacher asked a clever question.”

## Chapter 4 — The pot that did not explain everything

On the way home, rain began without warning.

They sheltered under a shop awning beside a woman arranging metal cooking pots. Some were small enough for one person's supper. Others looked capable of feeding a whole wedding party.

Fẹ́mi remembered the explanation about a source that never became empty.

He pointed at the largest pot. “Maybe that is why I like that version. You can picture it.”

“You can,” Zárà said. “A picture helps you remember a meaning.”

“Then perhaps the pot story proves how the name was invented.”

“Does it?”

He watched rainwater drip from the edge of the roof. The pot was a good image: something full, something enduring, something people depended upon. But his notebook still had no dated account of the first person who used that explanation, no original telling from a named community, and no clear record showing that this was how the sacred name first formed.

“Maybe not,” he admitted.

Zárà did not laugh at him.

“It can still matter to the person who taught it,” she said.

Fẹ́mi thought about how stories travel. A familiar image can help people carry a belief across generations. A word can gather prayers, memories, arguments and poetry around itself. Different communities or families can emphasise different things without agreeing on a single account of the word's earliest history.

Rain drummed against the roof.

If the pot was not a complete answer, perhaps it was a doorway into a better question:

**Who keeps telling this meaning, and why?**

## Chapter 5 — Three answers enter the classroom

On Monday Mrs Adéyẹmí asked for volunteers.

Fẹ́mi stood up with three pages instead of one.

He began with his cousin's answer: in many religious contexts the name points to the supreme divine source or creator.

Then he spoke about interpretations he had encountered: greatness, authority, constancy, and the image of a source that never runs dry. Some people divide the name into parts to explain those meanings, he said, but he could not honestly claim that the suggested breakdown was the proven historical origin.

Finally he described a different kind of question: the kind a linguist might ask about earlier forms of the word, pronunciation, tone marks, dialects and the ways sounds change.

A boy in the second row frowned. “So the search engine was wrong?”

“Not everything it showed was wrong,” Fẹ́mi replied. “Some explanations answer one question and pretend they answer three.”

That made the class laugh.

Mrs Adéyẹmí invited another student to speak. She said she had heard the name used differently in prayer at home. Another remembered a sermon. A third had seen a video but could not say who recorded it.

The teacher did not mark the student who mentioned the sermon as foolish. She did not pretend that the anonymous video was the same as a named person giving first-hand testimony.

Instead she asked them to explain the source of each account and what that source could actually tell them.

The blackboard filled with questions, arrows and names.

Fẹ́mi had arrived hoping to give the final answer.

He was beginning to enjoy the fact that the conversation had only just started.

## Chapter 6 — A notebook with room for another voice

When school ended, Zárà found Fẹ́mi sitting beneath a tree.

“What are you doing?”

“Fixing my notebook.”

He showed her the first page. The old heading had been crossed out so heavily that the paper was almost torn.

In its place he had written:

**OLÓDÙMARÈ: THE MEANINGS PEOPLE CARRY.**

Underneath he had drawn three columns.

The first was called **WHAT I HEARD**.

The second was **WHO OR WHERE I HEARD IT FROM**.

The third was **WHAT I STILL WANT TO ASK**.

He had left several pages blank.

“Why all the empty space?” Zárà asked.

“Someone else might explain it differently. I don't want to run out of room.”

Zárà smiled. “Even if you disagree with them?”

“Especially then. I'll need to understand what they mean before I decide which question to ask.”

He closed the notebook and remembered the pots in the rain, his aunt's warning, the cousin fighting with a charger, and the classroom full of different experiences.

There were names people shouted in anger, names whispered with love, names spoken in prayer, and names passed down as family memories.

A name could be important to someone long before an internet page explained it.

Fẹ́mi still wanted to know how Olódùmarè's name developed. He still wanted to examine the meanings people offered. But now he also wanted to hear the voices behind those meanings.

So at the bottom of the page he wrote one more question.

**What were you taught about this name?**

He did not draw a line beneath it.

Some questions are too interesting to close.
