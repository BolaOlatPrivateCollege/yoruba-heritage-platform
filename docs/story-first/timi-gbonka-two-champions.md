# Tìmì and Gbónká: The Duel That Would Not End

*An original imaginative retelling based on legendary motifs surrounding Tìmì Agbalẹ̀ and Gbónká in stories linked with Ṣàngó and royal rivalry. Accounts differ over the challengers, their fates, and the king's role. The witness Aṣàkẹ́, dialogue, settings and sequence below are invented for storytelling; no supernatural feat is asserted as a documented event.*

## Chapter 1 — Two names in a single breath

Whenever the royal drummer paused, somebody in the crowd shouted one of the two names.

"Tìmì!"

"Gbónká!"

The names bounced from one end of the open ground to the other until it seemed the town itself had chosen two different hearts.

Aṣàkẹ́ sold water near the gathering place. She had learned that whenever powerful men competed, thirsty people would pay for the privilege of watching. It was good business, but a poor reason to hope for trouble.

"Which one will win?" a boy asked her.

"Whichever one buys water," she said.

The boy frowned. He was certain that the question deserved a grander answer. Tìmì was said to stand firm when others faltered. Gbónká was said to be the kind of champion who walked towards a danger simply because someone told him it could not be overcome.

The trouble began with a careless boast.

A court messenger had praised Tìmì's courage during a public gathering. Another, wanting favour, had declared Gbónká even greater.

The court laughed. The two champions did not.

"Let the better man prove it," someone called.

Aṣàkẹ́ heard the cheer that followed and felt a chill. A joke had become a demand, and the crowd was too pleased with itself to see the difference.

## Chapter 2 — A king and a dangerous question

The palace hall was crowded by dawn.

On one side stood Tìmì, quiet and watchful. On the other stood Gbónká, whose expression gave away nothing. Courtiers filled every corner, waiting for the moment they could repeat later as proof that they had witnessed greatness.

The king listened to their names spoken with growing excitement.

"Why should strength fear a contest?" asked an adviser.

"Because a contest can become a command," another answered.

The king was not satisfied with the argument. He wanted a display that would remind the people of his court's power. Yet he also feared what might happen if either champion refused and the crowd took refusal for weakness.

"Will you stand against each other?" he asked.

Tìmì looked at Gbónká. They had once marched side by side, depending on one another when paths became dangerous.

"If we refuse," Tìmì said, "the singers may make our names small."

"If we agree," Gbónká replied, "they may sing about the wrong thing."

Nobody expected two champions to hesitate. The court had prepared drums, attendants and a place of honour for the victor. There was no seat for uncertainty.

Aṣàkẹ́ had been admitted to deliver water. She watched the nobles lean forward.

What was being tested? The courage of the champions—or the court's power to make men injure themselves for applause?

The king ordered the challenge to proceed.

## Chapter 3 — The fire everyone remembered

On the chosen day the spectators gathered around an open field.

The town would remember the fire long after it forgot who had ordered it lit. On that day, its flames were the boundary of a challenge that no ordinary contest could satisfy.

A line of flames burned at the far end of the ground. The heat made the air tremble. Nobody could see clearly through it.

Tìmì was told to approach first.

He stopped at the boundary and measured the distance. Some spectators shouted that he was afraid. Others insisted he was preparing an extraordinary feat that would be repeated in every marketplace.

Gbónká stepped forward. "The crowd wants a miracle."

"Then let the crowd perform one," Tìmì answered.

Gbónká almost smiled. "They will not like that."

The drummer struck three notes.

Smoke rose. A gust of wind scattered ash. For one terrifying moment the men disappeared from view. Aṣàkẹ́ dropped her water jug and ran towards the people nearest the flames, pulling them back before panic could spread.

When the smoke cleared, the champions were on opposite sides of the field.

The crowd erupted.

One group swore that Tìmì had walked through the fire untouched. Another claimed Gbónká had vanished and returned from beneath the earth. A third insisted neither had entered the flames at all.

Aṣàkẹ́ had seen almost nothing through the smoke.

By evening, everybody claimed to have witnessed the same impossible event—and nobody described it the same way.

## Chapter 4 — A victory that made no one happy

The court announced that a second challenge was needed.

"If the first proves nothing," said the king's adviser, "the next must be greater."

Aṣàkẹ́ heard the words while washing soot from her hands. She understood suddenly that no amount of spectacle would satisfy the people who wanted a final answer.

The champions met beside a quiet road away from the palace.

"Do you know how they tell it?" Tìmì asked.

"They say you are immortal," Gbónká replied.

"And they say you can command fire."

Gbónká looked towards the trees. "If we let them believe these things, what will they ask us to do next?"

Neither had a good answer.

At home, Aṣàkẹ́'s younger brother tried to leap over a cooking fire because the boys in the street had begun pretending to be champions. He stumbled and burned his hand. The injury was small, but it frightened her more than the great spectacle had.

She went to the palace and asked for an audience.

The official at the gate laughed. "What can a water seller tell the king about warriors?"

"How stories leave the field," she replied. "And what they make children think they can survive."

The official did not let her enter.

She waited until the palace attendants came out for water, then told them what had happened. The story reached the courtyard before she did.

## Chapter 5 — The last challenge

The final contest was announced beneath a burning sun.

The king took his place. The courtiers stood in bright cloth. Musicians prepared a rhythm fierce enough to make even reluctant spectators feel they were part of something historic.

Tìmì and Gbónká faced one another.

Aṣàkẹ́ looked across the crowd and saw her younger brother with his bandaged hand raised above the heads of the people. He was cheering for Gbónká. He had already forgotten what the fire had done to him.

The king lifted his arm.

Before he could give the signal, Tìmì spoke.

"We have both served this court. Must our final service be to prove that one of us is less than the other?"

The crowd hushed.

Gbónká drew a long breath. "If you call us brave only when we destroy each other, what name will you give those who bring us home?"

An adviser shouted for the challenge to continue. Some spectators booed. Others cheered at the unexpected words.

The king's face hardened.

The champions did not settle the question with a blow. They turned away from the centre of the field and stood together at its edge.

The refusal was not a victory without cost. Court favour could vanish. A king might feel humiliated. The crowd could make the story into a different accusation.

Aṣàkẹ́ knew the tale would be changed by evening.

Yet for the first time nobody could truthfully say the men had been forced to strike.

## Chapter 6 — What the singers kept

Years later, a travelling storyteller came to the market where Aṣàkẹ́ sold water.

He told of Tìmì and Gbónká, two champions whose rivalry had tested the limits of strength. In his version one passed through flames. In another version he had heard, a hero survived by astonishing means. Someone in the audience shouted that the tale was wrong and offered yet another ending.

The storyteller listened, then continued.

Aṣàkẹ́ smiled at the familiar pattern. Every audience believed the version it carried was the version that could not be changed.

The young seller beside her said, "You were there, weren't you?"

"In this telling I was."

"Then which man won?"

Aṣàkẹ́ poured water into a calabash.

"I saw two men. I saw fire. I saw a crowd eager to be amazed. Other tellers remember what happened next differently."

The boy looked disappointed. "That is not an ending."

"Then take this one."

She described the bandage around her brother's hand. She described how a boast in the palace became a demand in the square. She described the moment when everyone spoke louder than the two people who had been challenged.

The boy listened more closely than he had expected.

"What should the story teach us?"

"That is for listeners to argue about," she said. "But perhaps start with this: are the names Tìmì and Gbónká remembered because they frightened enemies, because they amazed a crowd, or because people needed a story large enough to contain their own hopes?"

The boy considered this, then asked whether he might repeat her version at the next gathering.

"Of course," she said. "But begin by telling them it is a version."

By dusk the market was full of song. The two names rose together in the voices of children, older listeners and travellers from distant roads.

"Tìmì!"

"Gbónká!"

Somewhere beyond the noise, other storytellers carried other endings. The story travelled farther than any champion could walk, changing at every place where it found a new voice.

And the duel, in that sense, never ended.
