# Ọya: Before the Wind Changed

*Original literary fiction inspired by Yorùbá traditions associating Ọya with wind, storms, transformation and courage. The town, characters, events and dialogue are imagined. This is not a transmitted sacred narrative, a ritual account or a historical reconstruction.*

## Chapter 1 — A voice in the dust

The morning the messenger arrived, Adérónkẹ́ was standing on the wall of the old courtyard, trying to catch a strip of cloth that had escaped from her mother's dyeing yard.

The wind caught the cloth again. It rose above the roofs, twisted like a living thing and disappeared.

Below her, a horse stopped at the gate. Its rider delivered news that travelled through the town faster than the wind itself. The neighbouring market had closed after a dispute between its leaders. Traders were turning towards Adérónkẹ́'s town, and the narrow streets would soon be crowded with people seeking somewhere to sell their goods.

Adérónkẹ́'s uncle, who supervised the market, was delighted.

"At last," he declared, "the world is coming to us."

Her mother, Ṣadé, looked towards the gate. "People are coming because something has gone wrong elsewhere. Remember that."

The difference between those two statements puzzled Adérónkẹ́. She had always wanted her town to become famous. She imagined new stalls, colourful cloth, strange foods and the sound of conversations from distant places.

By the evening, the first traders had arrived. One carried beautiful woven fabric. Another brought a bundle of damaged baskets. A woman travelling with two children asked where she could find somewhere to rest.

Adérónkẹ́ showed her the shaded courtyard. As they walked, the woman explained that she had left her own market without knowing whether she would ever recover her stall.

"Will everything be different here?" one of the children asked.

Adérónkẹ́ promised that it would be better. She made the promise before she knew what better would require.

## Chapter 2 — The woman nobody could command

The following day, Ṣadé found her daughter arguing with a boy who had refused to share his family's place beneath a shelter.

"You cannot order him to be generous," she said.

"Then what is the use of being brave?"

Ṣadé smiled. "Is bravery the same thing as getting your way?"

They returned to the dyeing yard, where cloth hung from wooden rails. While they worked, Ṣadé spoke of Ọya, whom Adérónkẹ́ had heard mentioned in family conversations about storms and change.

"My mother remembered Ọya in a particular way," she explained. "She spoke of strength that refused to be confined. Other communities have their own accounts, and I would not pretend to speak for them."

Adérónkẹ́ imagined a woman standing against a storm with her clothes streaming behind her. She wondered whether courage meant never being frightened.

Her mother shook her head.

"People sometimes admire powerful figures because they believe power makes uncertainty disappear. But what if courage means acting responsibly while uncertainty remains?"

Before Adérónkẹ́ could answer, a loud argument broke out near the market. A newly arrived trader had been told to move her goods from a space that had been offered to her only that morning.

Adérónkẹ́ hurried towards the voices. She wanted to settle the quarrel immediately and show that she was not afraid of anyone.

Ṣadé called after her.

"Find out what happened before you decide who must be blamed."

The wind lifted the cloth on the rails. This time Adérónkẹ́ noticed how much strength the slender wooden supports needed simply to keep it in place.

## Chapter 3 — The price of a loud voice

The traders had gathered around a young man named Délé. He claimed that outsiders were taking spaces reserved for local families. A woman named Síkírà insisted that she had already paid for her stall.

Adérónkẹ́ stepped between them and demanded that Délé apologise.

He laughed. "Were you there when the spaces were assigned?"

She had not been there, but she was too embarrassed to say so. She accused him of dishonesty. He accused her of favouritism. Soon other people were repeating fragments of their argument as though they had witnessed the whole dispute.

The market supervisor arrived and examined the records. Two people had been assigned the same place through an administrative mistake. Délé's family had a legitimate complaint. So did Síkírà.

Adérónkẹ́ felt the attention of the crowd settle on her.

She could have blamed the supervisor. She could have insisted that her intentions were good. Instead, she turned to Délé.

"I accused you without knowing what happened. I was wrong."

The apology did not restore friendship immediately. He remained angry, and Síkírà still needed a place to trade. But the supervisor was finally able to discuss the mistake without the crowd demanding that one person be declared a villain.

That evening Adérónkẹ́ sat beside the dyeing yard. She had imagined courage as a kind of fire that burned through opposition. Now she understood how easily her own anger could become another obstacle.

Her mother handed her a bowl of food.

"An apology does not make your voice smaller," Ṣadé said. "It makes people more willing to hear it next time."

Adérónkẹ́ ate in silence, thinking of the traders she had promised a better welcome.

## Chapter 4 — The night the roofs trembled

Towards sunset on the third day, the wind became unusually strong. Experienced market workers suspended trading and directed visitors towards appropriate shelter. The sky darkened, and loose cloth snapped violently above the stalls.

Adérónkẹ́ was helping her mother secure the dyeing yard when word arrived that several visiting families did not know where to go. The town's designated emergency helpers were already organising assistance.

Her first instinct was to rush into the market.

Ṣadé stopped her. "Do not create another person they have to rescue."

Instead, they made their sheltered courtyard available under the organisers' guidance. Adérónkẹ́ helped welcome the people directed there. She found the woman with the two children whom she had met at the gate, and offered them a dry place to sit.

The younger child asked whether the wind was angry.

Adérónkẹ́ remembered her mother's account of Ọya. She also remembered what her teacher had explained about storms.

"Different people understand the wind in different ways," she replied. "Right now, we need to stay somewhere safe."

The child leaned against her shoulder.

When the storm had passed, the courtyard was full of strangers. Some had lost goods. Others worried about damaged shelters. Nobody knew how quickly the market would reopen.

Délé appeared at the entrance with a neighbour. His family's stall had been damaged. Adérónkẹ́ offered him a seat without asking whether he had forgiven her.

He sat down.

For the first time since their quarrel, they spoke without an audience.

## Chapter 5 — The decision no one wanted

After inspections and repairs began, the market leaders faced an uncomfortable decision. There was not enough safe trading space for everyone to return to their original arrangements.

Some local traders wanted the visitors sent away. Some visitors feared that promises made to them would be forgotten. The supervisor proposed restoring the old allocation system as soon as possible.

Adérónkẹ́ asked permission to speak.

She described the duplicate stall assignment, the confusion at the gate and the families who had not known where to find assistance. She admitted her own mistake during the argument.

Then she asked a question that nobody had expected.

"If we rebuild everything exactly as it was, what will happen the next time the world changes?"

The meeting became quiet.

Síkírà suggested a transparent register of temporary trading spaces. Délé proposed that the local traders and visiting merchants choose representatives to discuss disagreements. Experienced workers explained which sections of the market could reopen safely.

There were objections, negotiations and revisions. No single suggestion solved everything. Yet the final plan was different from the old one because the people affected had been allowed to contribute.

Adérónkẹ́'s uncle looked at her as the gathering dispersed.

"You have made my work harder," he said.

She did not know whether he was angry until he smiled.

"And perhaps better."

That evening, she helped her mother take down the old damaged cloth from the rails. They would not try to make it look exactly as it had before. They would save what remained useful and begin something new.

## Chapter 6 — What the wind left behind

Weeks later, the market reopened in stages. The visiting traders who wished to stay were offered clearly recorded arrangements. Those who wanted to return home received information about the routes and conditions available to them.

Adérónkẹ́ noticed that the woman with the two children had decided to remain for another season. Délé helped her find someone who could repair a damaged basket. Síkírà waved to Adérónkẹ́ from a stall near the entrance.

The town had not become perfect. People still disagreed. Some visitors would eventually leave, and new problems would arise.

But Adérónkẹ́ no longer believed that change was something to conquer before it could touch her. She understood that a person could meet it with courage without pretending to control everybody around her.

At the dyeing yard, her mother handed her the edge of a newly finished cloth. Together they hung it on the repaired rails.

"Tell me again what your grandmother said about Ọya," Adérónkẹ́ asked.

Ṣadé repeated the family account as she remembered it. She reminded her daughter that another household might tell the story differently.

Adérónkẹ́ listened. She did not try to turn every word into a rule for everybody else.

Beyond the courtyard, the market was coming alive. Voices rose, baskets moved, and children ran towards the places where their families worked.

A gentle wind stirred the cloth.

This time Adérónkẹ́ did not climb the wall to chase it. She stood beside her mother, watching its movement, remembering the promises she had made and the lessons she had learned before the wind changed.
