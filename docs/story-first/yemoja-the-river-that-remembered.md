# Yemọja: The River That Remembered

*Original literary fiction inspired by Yorùbá traditions of Yemọja and her associations with water, motherhood and nurture. The community, flood, characters and dialogue are invented. The story is not a transmitted sacred narrative or an account of a particular historical event.*

## Chapter 1 — The house with two doorways

Every year, when the first heavy rains approached, Títí's grandmother asked her to help move the family's woven sleeping mats to the upper room. Every year Títí complained that the work was unnecessary.

Their house stood well above the water. The river looked so distant from their doorway that she could barely distinguish the people washing their vessels on its banks.

"Why do we prepare for something that is not happening?" she asked.

Bàbá Ṣẹ́gun, her grandmother's brother, pointed to a dark line across the wall of a nearby abandoned house. The mark was higher than Títí's head.

"Because it has happened before."

Títí looked at the mark and then at the quiet river. She wondered whether old people sometimes remembered things as larger than they really were.

Her grandmother, Yéjídé, brought out a small wooden chest containing letters, photographs and pieces of cloth. One photograph showed a group of people standing near a broad stretch of water. Títí recognised the old market tree behind them, but the river seemed to occupy land that was now covered by houses.

"That was the year your mother was born," Yéjídé explained. "Some families lived with us for many weeks."

Títí had never heard this story. She wanted to know why nobody had told her.

"Perhaps," her grandmother said, "we were waiting for you to ask."

That afternoon Títí began drawing a picture of the river as she knew it. She filled the banks with houses and drew the market tree at the centre. Everything looked permanent on paper.

Her grandmother watched her finish without saying a word.

## Chapter 2 — The names beneath the water

The following morning Títí went to the river with her friend Kẹ́hìndé. Their teacher had asked the class to collect accounts of changes in the community. Kẹ́hìndé intended to count new buildings. Títí decided to ask about places that no longer existed.

They met a basket maker who remembered a footpath beneath the present market. A trader described a small shelter that had once stood near the water. An elderly woman explained that a certain bend had been known by a different name when she was young.

Títí wrote everything down. Some of the descriptions appeared to contradict one another.

"Which person is right?" Kẹ́hìndé asked.

"Maybe they remember different years."

The basket maker overheard them and smiled. "The bank moved. The names moved with the people. You will need more than one drawing."

Later they reached a quiet stretch where Yéjídé was visiting a friend. The two women were speaking about Yemọja. Títí had heard the name many times, but she had never asked why the river mattered so much to them.

"In our family," her grandmother told her, "we remember Yemọja when we speak of the care that sustains life and the responsibilities we have to one another. Other families and communities have their own ways of remembering."

"Does the river remember everybody who has lived here?"

Yéjídé considered the question.

"The river leaves signs. People carry memories. Do not confuse the two, but do not ignore either."

Títí opened her notebook again. She drew the old footpath in a different colour from the new buildings.

For the first time her picture contained something she could not see.

## Chapter 3 — The night of many voices

Rain arrived in the late afternoon and continued into the evening. At first the sound was comforting. Títí sat beneath the veranda listening to drops strike the roof.

Then a neighbour came running with news that water had entered the lower part of the settlement. Adults began making calls and knocking on doors. The community's experienced emergency helpers directed people away from flooded paths and towards the designated gathering place.

Yéjídé told Títí to take the bag they had prepared. They left with other residents while their route remained safe, without returning for forgotten possessions.

At the gathering place, Títí found Kẹ́hìndé sitting beside his younger brother. The boy was frightened because he could not see their father in the crowd. A neighbour soon confirmed that their father was assisting at another safe gathering place, and Kẹ́hìndé relaxed.

Around them people spoke all at once. One woman needed dry clothes for her baby. An elderly man could not find his reading glasses. A trader worried about the goods she had left behind.

Títí wanted to help everyone. Instead, she asked the adults organising the shelter what she could safely do. They gave her a simple task: recording requests for supplies under their supervision, without announcing private details.

She moved from family to family, discovering that people needed different things. Some wanted food. Some needed news of relatives. Others simply wanted a quiet place to sit.

Her grandmother sat with a woman who had lost a treasured photograph. She did not promise that the photograph would be recovered. She listened.

Outside, the rain continued. Inside, people made room for one another.

## Chapter 4 — What could not be replaced

When authorities and experienced local helpers declared the affected areas safe to return to, the families began examining their losses. Títí accompanied her grandmother only to places they had been told were safe.

The river had left mud across several streets. In one courtyard a broken wooden chair lay upside down. People spoke softly as they discovered what remained of their homes.

Títí's own house had escaped the worst damage. The chest of family memories was dry.

She thought immediately of the woman who had lost her photograph at the shelter. Could another copy exist? She asked her grandmother whether they might help search.

Together they visited relatives and neighbours, asking whether anybody remembered the day the photograph had been taken. A cousin recalled a naming celebration. Another relative remembered the cloth worn by the woman in the picture.

Nobody found a copy.

Títí felt disappointed. She had imagined herself returning with the missing photograph and making everything right.

Instead, her grandmother asked the woman whether she would like to tell them about it. The woman described the people in the photograph, the courtyard where they had stood and the song someone had started singing just before the picture was taken.

With her permission, Títí wrote the account in her notebook and read it back to her. The woman corrected a name and added a detail about her mother's dress.

"It is not the same as the photograph," Títí said.

"No," the woman replied. "But now somebody else knows."

Títí understood that care did not always mean restoring what had been lost. Sometimes it meant respecting the loss and helping a person preserve what remained.

## Chapter 5 — A map made of memories

At school the teacher invited pupils to share their accounts of the community. Títí unfolded three drawings: the river as she had imagined it, the river as older residents remembered it and a new map showing places recently affected by the rains.

Her teacher explained that memories were valuable but could not replace measurements or professional assessments. The pupils labelled their sources and distinguished remembered boundaries from observed ones.

Kẹ́hìndé added the buildings he had counted. Other pupils brought stories about vanished paths, former gardens and families who had moved to higher ground.

With permission, they assembled copies of the accounts for a community exhibition. Nobody had to reveal a private experience. People could withdraw their contributions or correct mistakes.

Visitors lingered longest beside the drawing of the old market tree. Many remembered the tree, but not all remembered the same riverbank.

Yéjídé came to see the exhibition. Títí showed her the dark line she had drawn across the abandoned wall.

"I thought you were exaggerating," she admitted.

Her grandmother laughed. "A good question would have helped us both."

They watched a young father explain a flood he remembered to his daughter. Near them, two elderly women disagreed about the location of a former path. The teacher gave them separate labels rather than asking them to choose one version.

Títí thought of Yemọja and the different ways families understood water and nurture. She realised that remembering a sacred figure could mean far more than repeating a name. It could invite a conversation about how people cared for the living world and for one another.

## Chapter 6 — The river carries tomorrow

The following year, as the rainy season approached, Títí moved the sleeping mats without being asked.

She had also helped her family prepare their emergency contact information and familiarise themselves with the community's current safety guidance. The old photograph remained in the wooden chest, but a copy was now stored separately.

Her younger cousin arrived while she was drawing another picture of the river.

"Why does your map have so many colours?" he asked.

Títí explained that one colour represented places older residents remembered, another showed what people had observed recently, and another marked things they still needed to investigate.

"Which colour is the true one?"

"They answer different questions."

He pointed towards the water. "Tell me about Yemọja."

Títí put down her pencil. She told him what her grandmother had taught her, making clear that their family's understanding was not the only one. Then she told him about the night at the shelter and the woman whose photograph had been lost.

"Is that an old sacred story?" he asked.

"No. That happened to us. It helped me understand something my grandmother had been trying to teach me."

They walked with Yéjídé along a safe path overlooking the river. Their grandmother stopped to greet a neighbour. Títí watched the surface of the water moving beneath the afternoon light.

She no longer thought of the river as a line that could be drawn once and forgotten. It belonged to a changing landscape, surrounded by living memories and responsibilities.

Her cousin asked whether they could return the next day.

"Of course," Títí said. "But next time, you must bring your own notebook."

The river moved towards places beyond their view. Behind them, the village carried its memories forward.
