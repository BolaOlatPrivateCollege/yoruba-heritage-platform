# Ọ̀ṣun: When the World Forgot Her

*Original literary fiction inspired by Ọ̀ṣun's associations with rivers, care and feminine sacred authority. The settlement, people and events are imagined. This is not an authenticated Ifá verse, a transmitted sacred narrative or a historical account.*

## Chapter 1 — The empty chair

On the morning the village decided to build a new market, sixteen stools stood beneath the great tree. By noon every stool was occupied except the one that had been set aside for the waterkeepers.

Àyọ̀, the daughter of a cloth trader, had been asked to carry messages between the builders and the people who sold food. She watched the council unfold a drawing of their magnificent market. There would be a broad entrance, shaded stalls and a courtyard where visitors could rest. Everybody admired the drawing.

"Who will bring water?" she asked.

The man holding the drawing waved towards the river. "The river is there. It has always been there."

"But who will decide where people collect it?"

"We are discussing important matters today," another man said. "The women can arrange that later."

Àyọ̀ looked at the empty stool. Her aunt, Mórẹ́nikẹ́, had been invited to an earlier meeting, but nobody had sent for her this morning. For years she and the other waterkeepers had organised the collection of water and watched how the river changed with the seasons. They knew which banks were fragile, which paths flooded and when the shallow pools became unsafe.

By sunset the council had approved its plan. The drawing was carried through the village like a prize.

At home Àyọ̀ found her aunt sorting clay vessels beneath a veranda. She described the meeting, including the empty chair.

Mórẹ́nikẹ́ listened carefully. She did not shout or curse. She asked only one question.

"Did they leave room for the river in their drawing?"

Àyọ̀ could not remember seeing it.

## Chapter 2 — A market without water

The building began with great enthusiasm. Craftspeople arrived from neighbouring settlements. Traders chose their places, and children argued about which stalls would sell the sweetest fruit.

The first difficulty appeared when workers needed water for their ordinary tasks. The planned entrance occupied the safest path down to the river. People carrying heavy vessels had to make a longer journey over uneven ground.

A second difficulty appeared when the rains came. Water collected behind a wall that had been built across an old drainage channel. The courtyard became a shallow muddy basin. Several traders lost goods stored there.

The council called another meeting. A builder suggested raising the wall. A merchant suggested digging a deeper ditch. One elderly man declared that people simply needed to work harder.

Àyọ̀ raised her hand.

"My aunt says the river changes its course around that bend."

"Then ask your aunt to send somebody to clear the ditch," came the reply.

Àyọ̀ felt heat rising into her face. She had carried their messages faithfully for days. Yet every time she brought knowledge from the waterkeepers, somebody reduced it to a request for labour.

When she returned home, her aunt was helping a neighbour find a safer place to collect water. Àyọ̀ told her what had happened.

"They remember your hands when something needs carrying," she said bitterly. "They forget your mind when something needs deciding."

Mórẹ́nikẹ́ gave her a long look. "Then we must show them what their drawing cannot tell them. But we shall not risk anybody's safety to prove a point."

That evening they invited people who used the river every day to bring their memories to a gathering. Nobody was asked to agree with everybody else.

## Chapter 3 — The story beside the river

The gathering took place under a shelter near the water. An old potter remembered a flood from his childhood. A farmer described the season when a stream had disappeared beneath the earth. A woman who washed cloth explained why some stretches of bank were unsuitable for heavy foot traffic.

Àyọ̀ wrote down what they said. Where their memories differed, she kept both accounts.

When the conversation quietened, Mórẹ́nikẹ́ spoke of Ọ̀ṣun.

"My grandmother taught me to remember Ọ̀ṣun whenever I thought about the gifts of water and the importance of care," she told the gathering. "Other families tell different stories. I cannot speak for all of them."

In the family story she remembered, people had made elaborate plans without recognising the wisdom of a woman whose contribution they needed. They discovered that knowledge was not less valuable because it came from someone they had refused to consult.

Mórẹ́nikẹ́ did not claim that this explained every trouble in the market. She asked the listeners to consider their own behaviour.

"Sometimes we say we honour a person," she said, "but we do not allow that person to finish a sentence."

The potter nodded. He had been among the council members who approved the drawing.

"I thought the waterkeepers would object to the cost of moving the entrance," he admitted.

"You did not ask us," Mórẹ́nikẹ́ answered.

Nobody laughed at him. Instead, Àyọ̀ spread a copy of the market drawing on the ground. People gathered around it and began pointing out the places where their experience contradicted the plan.

For the first time, the drawing looked unfinished rather than magnificent.

## Chapter 4 — The day the market closed

A storm arrived before the council could discuss the new observations. Rain fell through the night. At dawn, the market courtyard was again flooded, and a section of the entrance had become unsafe.

The traders closed the market. Nobody was hurt, but the loss of another trading day frightened people who depended on their daily earnings.

The council summoned workers. This time the potter interrupted before any instructions were given.

"We need the people who understand this place," he said.

The council sent for Mórẹ́nikẹ́ and the other waterkeepers. When they arrived, someone offered Mórẹ́nikẹ́ a stool at the edge of the gathering.

She remained standing.

"My colleagues need seats too. The farmer knows the upper stream. The cloth workers know the bank. The builders know what their walls can bear. Let everybody who has knowledge speak."

Àyọ̀ waited for an argument. Instead, the potter stood up and moved his own stool to make room.

Together they examined the drawing. The experienced builders explained which changes were possible. The waterkeepers identified safer access points. Traders explained what the delay was costing their households.

There was disagreement. Moving an entrance would take time. Some stalls would need to be reassigned. But the disagreement was now about a real problem rather than about whose voice mattered.

Before they left, a council member asked Mórẹ́nikẹ́ what offering would make everything right.

She replied that sacred observances belonged to the people entrusted with them, and that the council still had responsibilities it could not hand away.

"You have heard what is wrong with your market," she said. "Do not forget it tomorrow."

## Chapter 5 — What the women brought

The revised market did not emerge in a single afternoon. The settlement arranged for experienced workers to inspect the damaged areas and plan the necessary repairs. Traders found temporary places to sell their goods. People shared news about safe routes and helped neighbours who could not carry their own loads.

Àyọ̀ was given a new responsibility: recording decisions and returning to anyone whose concerns had not been answered.

She discovered that the waterkeepers were not a group with one opinion. Some wanted a shorter path to the water. Others cared most about keeping the crowded market away from fragile banks. One proposed a resting place for older people. Another reminded the council that children also used the paths.

They debated. They changed their minds. They asked the builders questions.

What they brought was not a mysterious ability to agree about everything. It was knowledge gained through years of work that the first council had overlooked.

When the repairs were complete and the market was judged ready to reopen, people walked through the entrance without blocking the ordinary route to the river. The courtyard was arranged with the water's seasonal movement in mind. The village had a plan for looking after the new space together.

At the reopening, a man praised the council for its wisdom. The potter shook his head.

"Praise the people who corrected us," he said.

Àyọ̀ watched her aunt smile. It was not the triumphant smile of someone who had defeated an enemy. It was the quiet satisfaction of someone whose work would now be recognised.

## Chapter 6 — The seventeenth stool

Months later another meeting was called beneath the great tree. Àyọ̀ arrived early and counted the stools.

There were seventeen.

The newest stood near the centre, not at the edge. A child sitting beside his grandmother asked whether it belonged to a queen.

"It belongs to the person whose knowledge we have not yet heard," the potter explained.

Àyọ̀ liked that answer, though she knew one extra stool would never be enough by itself. People could still be invited and ignored. They could still be praised and denied the power to decide.

Her aunt sat beside her as the meeting began.

A young farmer described a concern about the road. A trader asked a question about the market. When a girl raised her hand to speak about the river path, the council waited for her to finish.

Afterwards Àyọ̀ and Mórẹ́nikẹ́ walked towards the water. The evening light lay across its surface, and the sounds of the market drifted from behind them.

"Will they always remember?" Àyọ̀ asked.

"No," her aunt replied. "Remembering is something people must practise."

Àyọ̀ thought of the family account of Ọ̀ṣun she had heard under the shelter. She imagined telling it to a younger cousin, alongside the story of the market. She would make clear which was the inherited memory her aunt had described and which was the adventure they had lived in their own little settlement.

She would tell them about the empty chair, the drawing without a river and the people who had learned to listen.

And she would ask them, when they made plans of their own, to look around the gathering before congratulating themselves.

Who was missing? Whose knowledge had they forgotten?

The river moved on, reflecting the last of the light.
