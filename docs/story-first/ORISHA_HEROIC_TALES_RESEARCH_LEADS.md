# Òrìṣà Legends & Heroic Tales — additional editorial research leads

**Prepared 23 November 2026 · PRIVATE RESEARCH DOSSIER · not reader-facing publication.**

These three topics were proposed by the project owner for the universal-audience Àṣà & Ìtàn Yorùbá website. Keep them separate from the earlier five mysterious-story leads in `YORUBA_MYSTERY_RESEARCH_LEADS.md`. This dossier does not claim a first-hand ritual interview, invented elder testimony, or that any single telling is universally canonical.

**Research rule:** Do not use Samuel Johnson's books or online accounts dependent on them as factual foundations. Prioritise current community custodians, respected cultural practitioners, locally based oral-history fieldwork, art objects and separately verified scholarship. In reader-facing Beliefs & Figures/Stories, explain who tells a version rather than issuing spiritual truth verdicts. Scholarship and disputed chronology belong under Research.

## 1. Ṣàngó — thunder, royal memory and living devotion

**Classification:** living sacred òrìṣà traditions and overlapping royal legends of Ọ̀yọ́, **not** simply an invented superhero, a single fixed romance, or an independently established royal chronology.

**Grounded elements:**
- The Smithsonian National Museum of African Art identifies the `oshe Ṣàngó` dance staff and double-headed axe imagery; it associates Ṣàngó with thunder and accounts of a deified legendary king of old Ọ̀yọ́: https://africa.si.edu/collection/object/nmafa_86-12-11
- The University of Michigan Museum of Art notes a carved figure in an `oshe Ṣàngó` may represent a **devotee**, not Ṣàngó himself; its double-axe motif is associated with so-called thunderstones: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/dance-staff-2001-2-22/
- Sainsbury Centre describes the double axe and thunderstone imagery in ritual material culture, illustrating that symbols have multiple contexts beyond a literal handheld weapon: https://sainsburycentre.ac.uk/art-and-objects/227-shango-staff-in-the-form-of-a-woman/
- The Detroit Institute of Arts discusses ritual songs and the tensions between Ṣàngó's temper and composure in the depiction: https://dia.org/collection/shango-wand/65728
- Smithsonian staff catalogue provides independent artifact descriptions: https://www.si.edu/object/staff%3Anmafa_86-12-11

**On Ọya, Ọ̀ṣun, and Ọ̀bà:** Their interrelationships with Ṣàngó occur in different Yorùbá oral and religious tellings. Do not present all three as mere supporting characters or declare one universal marriage sequence. Explore their own roles and individual traditions in separate profiles, consulting community-specific practitioners before interpreting sacred stories. A current UNESCO document mentions these relationships and river accounts; verify the cited local origin and context before any precise assertion: https://whc.unesco.org/document/224879 .

**Story direction:** *When the Thunder Spoke Twice* — a wholly original fictional intergenerational mystery about a misunderstood storm sign, a ritual emblem seen from two perspectives and the difference between a visitor's interpretation and a local family's explanation. If Ṣàngó is depicted as a speaking character, obtain contextual review by an appropriate Yorùbá practitioner; avoid turning restricted liturgy into invented ritual directions. Clearly label imaginative fiction and any narrated community version independently.

**Before a long-form adaptation:** request permissioned local accounts about Ṣàngó from Ọ̀yọ́-area custodians and practitioners; record their exact community, terms and credit preferences without publicly disclosing sensitive locations or practices.

## 2. Mọ́remí Àjàṣorò — courage and the Ẹ̀dì festival of Ilé-Ifẹ̀

**Classification:** living **commemorative heroic tradition** and historical-memory narratives. Accounts of spying/infiltration, opposing raiders, sacrifice and royal genealogy differ and cannot automatically be presented as independently corroborated historical events with precise dates.

**Community/fieldwork leads:**
- *Indigenous Ritual Performance in Ile-Ife: A Further Light into the Edì Festival* (Redeemer's University repository, 2017) reports fieldwork in Ilé-Ifẹ̀ and oral sources, specifically discussing Mọ́remí and the Edì festival: https://repository.run.edu.ng/items/6a190a36-6724-45ac-a3de-8c2ab6ea349b
- *Edi Festival in Ipetumodu: A Consequent Emotion and Socio-Religious Phenomenon* (2012) draws on observation and oral interviews, showing nearby communities have their own festival contexts: https://www.richtmann.org/journal/index.php/jesr/article/view/11850
- Queen Moremi Ajasoro International, an association connected with the Ifẹ̀ commemorative heritage, explains the Ẹ̀dì festival within its own community perspective: https://qmainternational.org/edi/
- A 2026 local report documents the contemporary Ẹ̀dì celebration and ceremonial custodians: https://tribuneonlineng.com/edi-festival-how-ile-ife-keeps-the-spirit-of-moremi-alive/

**Important historical sensitivity:** Some accounts use “Igbo” for raiders in the legend. Do not equate this automatically with today's Igbo ethnic group or present a legend as proof of ethnic hostility. Preserve the wording of a particular attributed version with historical/geographical qualification from actual Ifẹ̀ tellers. Avoid invented ancient dates, personally identifying current custodians without consent, or staging restricted rites as though they were documentary fact.

**Story direction:** *The Woman Who Asked What the Shadows Hid* — an original framed historical fiction set around a fictional contemporary family's experience of how Mọ́remí is remembered during the Ẹ̀dì period. Make the commemorative tradition visible and attribute the local perspective, while **not** fictionalising a first-hand palace interview or claiming invented dialogue reproduces the queen's own speech.

**Before publication:** have an Ilé-Ifẹ̀ cultural custodian examine the account; distinguish real present-day Ẹ̀dì practices and community memory from dramatised historical episodes. Commission an original reader version only after a specific telling has a source and adaptation rights.

## 3. Olúrónbí and Irókò — the vow that could not be taken back

**Classification:** enduring Yorùbá folktale with **multiple circulating narrative versions**; not necessarily a fixed claim that the vow was made for infertility or motherhood. Some versions concern success in trade, others childbirth; the child, ending and role of Irókò/Olúwéré also vary.

**Version comparison:**
- An older published folktale collection, `Yoruba Legends` (M. I. Ogumefu) includes an Oluronbi/Iroko account. Source transcription to be cross-checked for exact attribution, rights and original community: https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/african/yoruba-and-ife/yoruba-legends
- Yoruba Tales publishes an independent modern `Olurombi: An Iroko Tale` version, with a child called Olúrónkẹ́ and varying motivations. Respect publisher's rights: https://yorubatales.com/2-olurombi/
- OldNaija's 2018 public account centres trade/market gains and a girl whose name varies by version: https://oldnaija.com/2018/09/06/the-story-of-oluronbi-and-iroko-oluwere-the-spirit-tree/
- A review of the 2013 Genii Games interpretation notes an alteration in the sex of the tree spirit and an optimistic ending, demonstrating contemporary adaptation rather than one exclusive canon: https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/adebayo-adegbembo/oluronbi/
- A cultural-literary academic discussion of Irókò traditions identifies the story's role in perceptions of the tree, but its specific account must be distinguished from present-day practitioner belief: https://pgsds.ictp.it/xmlui/bitstream/handle/123456789/582/Dr%20Olugbamigbe%20Thesis%20Ph.D..pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1

**Editorial guardrails:** Treat Irókò sacred significance respectfully. A folktale about vows need not be an instruction or endorsement of giving a child in sacrifice. Do not depict child endangerment graphically or use the story to stigmatise infertility or motherhood. Maintain multiple possible endings and explain whose version is being presented.

**Story direction:** *The Promise Beneath the Irókò* — first publish an attributed, licensed **version guide**, then a newly commissioned original retelling after consent/rights review. Alternatively, craft a fully distinct original tale about a different promise and its consequences without reproducing a contemporary adaptation's specific characters or dialogue.

**Yorùbá spelling note:** Verify tonal spellings of Ṣàngó, Ọ̀ṣun, Ọya, Ọ̀bà, Mọ́remí, Ẹ̀dì, Olúrónbí and Irókò with a proficient proofreader and, where appropriate, a tradition bearer; do not impose an unverified tone pattern on a person's preferred orthography. English search aliases (Shango, Oshun, Moremi, Oluronbi) remain useful search metadata, not substitutes for checked display names.
