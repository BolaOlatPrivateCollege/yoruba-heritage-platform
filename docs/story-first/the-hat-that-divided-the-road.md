# The Hat That Divided the Road

*An original imaginative retelling inspired by a widely circulating Yorùbá Èṣù tale about different viewpoints.*

**For readers:** The two farmers, their names, dialogue and the details in this version are invented. Accounts of Èṣù and a differently coloured hat have been collected in several tellings; this is not the authentic words of a named community storyteller, an Ifá verse, or a description of one documented ceremony. Different communities may tell it differently.

## Chapter 1 — Two friends and one narrow path

Everyone knew that Adé and Bọ́lá were friends.

They had worked neighbouring farms for so long that people joked the boundary between their land existed only because the crops needed somewhere to stop. When the rain was late, they worried together. When the first green shoots appeared, they celebrated together. When Adé's goat ate half a basket of Bọ́lá's vegetables, they argued for one afternoon and laughed about it the next.

The path between their fields was narrow enough for one person to walk along comfortably. It ran past an old tree and disappeared towards the market road. On busy days, buyers used it as a shortcut.

That path was where the trouble began.

One morning Bọ́lá arrived carrying a broken hoe. Adé lifted his head from the soil.

“Let me guess,” Adé said. “Your hoe has decided to retire before you do.”

Bọ́lá sighed. “If you spent less time making jokes, you might help me repair it.”

Adé left his work, examined the tool and promised to lend Bọ́lá his spare. Before noon they had forgotten the disagreement. At least, that was what Adé believed.

For three days, another worry had been troubling them. A patch of land near the path was producing better crops than either expected. A few people at the market had begun asking whose harvest it really was.

“It has always been my side,” Adé said.

“Only when you stand where you stand,” Bọ́lá replied.

They said it lightly. Yet each time the subject returned, the laughter arrived a little later.

That afternoon an unfamiliar traveller appeared at the far end of the path.

Neither friend noticed him at first.

## Chapter 2 — A stranger walks between them

Adé was on the left, clearing weeds from a row of yams. Bọ́lá was on the right, counting the seedlings that had survived the dry spell.

The traveller wore plain clothes and walked without hurry. His hat, however, was difficult to miss. From Adé's side it looked bright red, as vivid as a strip of cloth in the afternoon sun. Bọ́lá saw the other side: white, clean and dazzling.

The traveller passed between the two farmers.

Adé straightened. “Good afternoon!”

The stranger turned his head slightly but did not stop. Bọ́lá offered a greeting too. Soon the traveller was gone beyond the bend.

Adé watched him disappear. “That was a splendid red hat.”

“Red?” Bọ́lá laughed. “You need to clean the dust from your eyes. The hat was white.”

“I saw it clearly.”

“So did I.”

Adé planted his hoe in the soil. “Perhaps you saw a different traveller.”

“One traveller came along this path. One.”

“Then you must have mistaken the colour.”

Bọ́lá stood and pointed towards the bend. “He passed right beside me. Do you think I cannot recognise white?”

Adé's smile vanished.

A small argument should have ended there. The traveller had gone. The colour of a stranger's hat could not water crops or fix a hoe. But neither man could bear to be told that his own eyes were unreliable.

They had spent years trusting one another with seeds, tools and difficult seasons.

Now neither trusted the other about a hat.

## Chapter 3 — The argument grows legs

At the market the next day, Adé told the story to a woman selling peppers.

“A man came past wearing a red hat,” he said. “Bọ́lá insists it was white.”

The pepper seller tilted her head. “Did you both stand on the same side of him?”

Adé waved the question away. “What difference would that make?”

By the time Bọ́lá reached the market, the story had changed. Someone said the friends were quarrelling about a stranger who had insulted one of them. Another said the quarrel was really about the patch of fertile land. A third claimed the traveller had worn no hat at all.

Bọ́lá found Adé beside the grain stall.

“So you've been telling people I am foolish?”

“I said nothing of the kind.”

“Then why is everybody repeating it?”

Adé had no answer. He had told a story about a hat; the market had supplied all the missing words.

The two friends walked home separately.

For the first time in years, Adé did not leave his spare hoe by the shared path. Bọ́lá did not bring the basket of vegetables he usually gave Adé's family.

Soon the narrow path felt wider than a river.

Children noticed. Buyers noticed. Even the goats seemed confused when they approached the boundary and found the two men looking away from each other.

There are quarrels that begin with a broken promise. There are quarrels that begin with a stolen belonging.

This one began with two men telling the truth about what they had seen—and refusing to believe that the other man might also be telling the truth.

## Chapter 4 — The second crossing

Several days passed.

Adé worked late, partly because he needed to, partly because going home meant admitting he had nobody to talk to. Bọ́lá worked just as late on the other side.

Then, at the hour when the shadows grow long, a familiar figure appeared at the bend.

The traveller was returning.

Adé recognised the clothes. Bọ́lá recognised the walking stick. Both recognised the hat.

But now the traveller approached from the opposite direction.

Bọ́lá saw red.

Adé saw white.

They shouted at the same time.

“Look! The hat is red!”

“Look! The hat is white!”

Then they stopped.

Something had changed, and it was not the hat.

The traveller stood between them. For a moment neither man spoke.

Adé moved carefully towards the path. Bọ́lá stepped nearer from his side. They walked around the traveller and looked from one side, then the other. Red on one half. White on the other.

Adé felt foolish. Bọ́lá looked as though he wanted to argue with the earth beneath his feet.

“You thought I was lying,” Bọ́lá said quietly.

“I thought you were wrong,” Adé replied.

“Is there much difference when you say it loudly enough?”

Adé had never considered that question.

The traveller did not offer them an easy escape from their embarrassment. He had made no claim about the colour. He had simply walked down a path while two men supplied their own certainty.

Bọ́lá studied the two halves of the hat.

“And all this time,” he whispered, “we never walked around to the other side.”

## Chapter 5 — Who was the traveller?

That evening Adé's grandmother listened to the account while the two farmers sat together for the first time in days.

She asked about the path. She asked who had been standing where. She asked how a stranger had appeared at exactly the moment when the friends were beginning to quarrel over their boundary.

Finally she said, “Some tellings of this sort of tale name the traveller Èṣù.”

Adé had heard the name before. So had Bọ́lá. But they had grown up hearing it in very different situations.

One person had used it when speaking of a powerful messenger and the consequences of choices. Someone else had used it as another word for the Christian devil. Others had repeated warnings or jokes without explaining where they came from.

The grandmother did not pretend that one sentence could settle every religious question.

“Different families explain these stories in different ways,” she said. “If you want to know what a particular telling means, find out who tells it and in what tradition. But what did *you* learn from the road?”

Bọ́lá looked at Adé. “That your eyes can be right and your conclusion still incomplete.”

Adé nodded slowly.

“And that the road between us had two sides before either of us cared to notice.”

The grandmother smiled, but the smile did not end the conversation. She asked them one more question.

“What if the hat had three colours?”

Neither answered immediately.

They had spent a week defending just one.

## Chapter 6 — A path for two people

At sunrise, Adé carried his spare hoe across the path and placed it beside Bọ́lá's shed.

Bọ́lá pretended not to see him.

Then he brought two baskets of vegetables and set one beside Adé's door.

Neither gave a grand speech. The silence was enough at first.

Later they went together to examine the fertile patch of land. They measured its edges and asked a neighbour to help them agree on where the boundary lay. There was nothing magical about the work. It was slow, practical and a little embarrassing.

For the first time, they were willing to look at the same ground from both sides.

At the market, somebody asked whether the stranger had truly been Èṣù.

“I cannot tell you what every storyteller would say,” Bọ́lá answered. “I can tell you how we argued.”

Adé laughed. “And how we nearly lost a friendship over a hat we had not bothered to examine.”

An older woman nearby said she had heard a version with different colours. Another man remembered a telling in which the traveller wore more than two.

For once, Adé did not interrupt to correct them.

He asked them to continue.

Because perhaps the real mystery was not the colour of a hat. Perhaps it was how two people could stand so close, look so carefully and still miss something that lay just beyond their own side of the road.

That evening Adé and Bọ́lá walked home together.

The path between their fields was no wider than before.

But it had room for two.
