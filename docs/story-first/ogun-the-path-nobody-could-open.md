# Ògún: The Path Nobody Could Open

*Original literary fiction inspired by Yorùbá traditions associating Ògún with iron, skilled work and opening paths. All characters, conversations and events in this tale are imagined, not a transmitted sacred narrative or a historical reconstruction.*

## Chapter 1 — The road that disappeared

By the third morning after the rains, the people of the village had stopped asking when the road would open. They had begun asking who would be the first to leave.

A fallen tree lay across the passage to the market. Beyond it, roots and earth had collapsed into a deep gully. No basket of vegetables could pass; no merchant could bring back salt. The forest seemed to have swallowed the road while everyone slept.

Adéwálé, the apprentice at the forge, watched his mother turn over the last of her yams. She had promised to sell them before they spoiled. He knew the sound of her silence better than the sound of her voice.

"I can clear it," he announced.

His sister, Bísí, looked up from repairing a basket. "And the gully?"

"I will find a way across."

"Then find it before you promise."

He went to the forge, where his teacher, Bàbá Fálọlá, was sharpening a hoe. On the wall hung tools that had taken days to make, and each bore a little mark identifying its maker. Adéwálé loved to imagine his own mark on a blade that would be known throughout the village.

"Give me the strongest cutlass," he said. "The whole village will know who opened the road."

The blacksmith set down the hoe. "A road is not a trophy."

Outside, another trader turned home with a basket still balanced on her head. Adéwálé was too impatient to ask what his teacher meant.

## Chapter 2 — The stranger at the forge

That evening a traveller appeared at the forge with a damaged farming tool. His clothes were dusty, his hands tired. He did not ask for food or praise; he asked whether the tool could be repaired.

Adéwálé examined it. "It would be quicker to make a new one."

"Quicker for whom?" the traveller asked. "I have no money for another."

The apprentice looked towards the forest. Every hour spent on this man's little problem was an hour lost from his great plan.

Bàbá Fálọlá took the damaged tool and showed him how much usable material remained. He reminded Adéwálé that the owner had worked with it for years. Its value was not measured only by the quantity of iron it contained.

Together they repaired it. When the traveller picked it up, he smiled for the first time.

"My father used to tell me that the strength of a tool lies in the work it makes possible," he said. "He spoke of Ògún whenever he taught us to respect iron."

Adéwálé had heard people speak of Ògún at home and at the forge. Some remembered the opening of paths; others emphasised the responsibilities of those who work with metal. His teacher had always insisted that a dangerous tool deserved respect, however beautifully it had been made.

"Do you know the forest road?" Adéwálé asked.

"I know a little of it," the traveller answered. "But the road you remember may not be the road that remains."

The words irritated him. He wanted a solution, not another question. Yet he noticed that the stranger walked with the careful attention of someone who had learned the cost of haste.

## Chapter 3 — A blade is not an answer

At sunrise Adéwálé arrived at the broken road with a group of young volunteers. He showed them the cutlass he had borrowed and declared that they would be back before the afternoon meal.

For a while the work seemed easy. Branches were moved out of the passage, and the village came into view through the leaves. Someone began singing. Someone else called Adéwálé a hero.

Then Bísí, who had followed with drinking water, noticed that the ground beside the fallen tree was shifting.

"Stop," she called.

Adéwálé pretended not to hear. The remaining branch was almost loose. One more effort, he thought, and everyone would see that he had been right.

A section of wet earth slid into the gully. The branch moved with it. The volunteers jumped away. A basket vanished into the muddy water below.

For a long time nobody spoke.

Bísí was the first to count the people. Everyone was safe, but only just. The old tree now leaned across the gap at an angle that made the passage even more dangerous.

"You were right about the branches," she said at last. "You were wrong about the ground."

He hated hearing the two sentences together.

When they returned, Bàbá Fálọlá listened without interrupting. Adéwálé expected punishment, but his teacher asked him to describe what he had seen.

"The earth moved."

"Before or after the branch?"

"Before."

"And what did your sister see that you did not?"

Adéwálé stared at the floor. He had wanted to prove that iron could solve the problem. Instead, he had discovered that a fine blade could become useless in the hands of someone who refused to listen.

## Chapter 4 — What the forest knew

The next day, Adéwálé asked the traveller to accompany him. Bísí came too, carrying a notebook in which she had drawn the broken passage.

They did not bring cutting tools.

At the edge of the forest, they met a woman named Mọ́júbà, who gathered leaves and knew the seasonal streams. She studied Bísí's drawing and pointed to a place the apprentice had ignored.

"Water travels downhill whether you are watching or not," she said. "If you put your road in its way, it will answer you again next rainy season."

She took them towards higher ground. The old passage had been convenient when the village was small, but years of changing watercourses had weakened it. An older footpath curved around the dangerous section, though it was narrow and overgrown.

Bísí marked it on her drawing. The traveller asked where the new route might join the market road. Adéwálé walked behind them, noticing signs he had overlooked the first time.

By evening they returned with a proposal: the dangerous gully should be closed, and the villagers should assess the higher route together. The elders arranged for experienced workers to examine it before anyone started clearing.

At the forge, Adéwálé told his teacher that he might not be the person who opened the road after all.

Bàbá Fálọlá laughed. "You might be one of the people."

That distinction had once seemed small to him. Now it felt as wide as the forest.

He helped prepare the tools requested by the workers. The blades would serve a plan made with knowledge of the land, not replace that knowledge.

## Chapter 5 — The hands that opened the way

The work took longer than the apprentice had promised. Experienced workers organised the clearing, keeping everyone away from the damaged ground. Farmers explained where carts could pass. Traders described how much space their loads required. Bísí kept the drawing up to date as the route changed.

Adéwálé spent most mornings at the forge. A damaged hoe arrived, then another. A handle broke. People needed implements that suited different kinds of work. He learned to listen to the person describing a task before deciding what tool to prepare.

One afternoon a boy asked why the apprentice did not walk at the front of the workers like a great warrior.

"Because they need me here," he said.

He surprised himself by how easily the answer came.

When the new route was finally declared ready for ordinary travellers, the village gathered at its entrance. The first woman to walk through carried a basket of vegetables. Adéwálé recognised her from the morning he had made his boast.

She stopped to thank the workers, the people who brought water, the elders who organised the effort and the craftworkers who repaired the tools.

The traveller stood beside the blacksmith. "Now you understand why my father remembered Ògún at the forge," he said.

Adéwálé asked him to explain.

"In the account I learned, clearing a path was never a reason to despise the people who would walk on it."

The apprentice thought of the days when he had wanted his name to be the only name remembered. He looked at Bísí, holding her muddy drawing, and understood how foolish that ambition had been.

## Chapter 6 — The mark on the handle

Market day arrived. The village woke before dawn to the sounds of baskets, footsteps and voices. Adéwálé helped his mother carry her yams to the entrance of the new passage.

There, someone had placed a simple sign directing travellers away from the old gully. Beside it was a bench for people who needed to rest.

His mother noticed him looking at the bench. "You made the tools for that?"

"Some of them," he replied. "Other people made the bench."

She smiled. "Then you have learned something."

Later, when he returned to the forge, Bàbá Fálọlá handed him a newly repaired hoe and invited him to put his own maker's mark on it.

Adéwálé hesitated. He had waited months for this moment. Yet the mark no longer meant that he was greater than the other apprentices. It meant someone would trust the work of his hands.

He made the little mark carefully.

When a neighbour came for the hoe, he explained how it had been repaired and asked her to return if anything felt wrong. She thanked him and went on her way.

Bísí arrived in the evening. She wanted to know whether he would tell the story of the new road to their children one day.

"Of course," he said.

"And who will be the hero?"

He looked towards the forest, where traders were returning with empty baskets and full conversations.

"The road," he answered, laughing. "And everybody who made it possible."

The forge grew quiet. Its last light fell on the tools waiting for tomorrow's work. Adéwálé no longer imagined iron as an answer to every problem. He imagined the hands that made things, the minds that guided them, and the people whose journeys became possible because someone had learned to work well.

That was the path he had not known how to open.
