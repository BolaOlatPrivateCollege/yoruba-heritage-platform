# Ṣàngó: When Thunder Answered

*An original framed literary telling inspired by Yorùbá accounts of Ṣàngó's thunder, royal power and living sacred importance. Different Ọ̀yọ́ and practitioner traditions remember Ṣàngó differently. The festival audience and characters in the framing story are invented; this is not a historical reconstruction, a sacred verse, a ritual guide or the words of an actual diviner.*

## Chapter 1 — The sky that stopped the drums

The first thunderclap split the afternoon before the drummer's hand touched the skin.

Children scattered from the square. Sellers snatched covers over their baskets. A woman carrying a bowl of kola nuts stopped so suddenly that the man behind her almost fell into the dust.

At the edge of the crowd, Kúnlé laughed.

"Only rain," he said. "Why does everybody look as though a king has arrived?"

His aunt, Àdìkẹ́, who had spent the morning helping arrange a community storytelling gathering, looked at him with narrowed eyes.

"Who told you thunder is only one thing to everybody?"

"The clouds," Kúnlé said. "They seem clear enough."

He was visiting from a distant town and prided himself on never accepting a story until somebody explained it. Àdìkẹ́ respected questions. She did not respect the habit of using them to end conversations before they began.

Under a covered veranda stood an old ceremonial object on display with permission from its custodian: a carved double-axe form associated with Ṣàngó. Kúnlé had seen photographs of such objects before. The two heads of the emblem made him think of a blade that could cut in opposite directions.

"Was that what the king used in battle?" he asked.

"A question worth asking," Àdìkẹ́ replied. "But first, are you asking about a royal story, a sacred emblem or an object carved by someone's hands?"

Kúnlé stared at her. He had expected one answer.

Across the square, a second thunderclap rolled. This time the drums began at once, not as an attempt to command the sky but as part of the gathering that the storm had interrupted.

An elderly storyteller named in this fiction as Bàbá Délé drew a circle beneath the veranda.

"Come," he said. "There is room to hear more than one telling."

## Chapter 2 — A crown too heavy for one head

Bàbá Délé began with a king.

"Some accounts speak of Ṣàngó as a powerful ruler remembered in the old Ọ̀yọ́ world. The details differ, and they come through generations of tellers. For tonight, imagine a royal court where every decision is heard twice: first by the people who are pleased, and then by those who pay the price."

In his imagined court, messengers waited beneath the eaves. A council had gathered because two villages disputed a road that carried traders between them. Both claimed they had cleared it. Both believed the taxes collected there belonged to them.

The king's advisers gave contradictory advice.

"Make the stronger village pay the weaker," one urged.

"Reward those who fought to keep the road open," said another.

A third asked whether anyone had walked the road recently.

The king became impatient. Each speaker wanted him to prove his strength by deciding quickly. The hall was full of voices, and every voice sounded certain.

Then a young courier entered with dust on his feet.

"I walked the road this morning," he said.

The courtiers laughed at the simplicity of his statement.

"Then speak," the king commanded.

"The bridge is broken," the courier replied. "Neither village can carry goods across it."

In the veranda, Kúnlé smiled despite himself.

"That is not a thunder story," he objected.

"Not yet," Bàbá Délé said. "But it is a story about power. Tell me what happens when power becomes more concerned with being obeyed than with listening."

Outside, rain struck the leaves in a rush. Àdìkẹ́ moved a bowl beneath a leak and waited for the answer.

## Chapter 3 — The noise inside the palace

In the storyteller's imagined court, the broken bridge became an embarrassment. Instead of sending builders immediately, officials argued over who should have warned the king sooner.

The king struck the floor with his staff.

"Enough!"

A crash of thunder answered beyond the palace roof.

Nobody in the room knew whether the storm had arrived by chance or whether a story about that moment would one day give it a deeper meaning. But the courtiers fell silent. Their faces showed fear, and the king saw how quickly a raised voice could empty a room of honest advice.

That night the courier returned with another message. People had begun to repair the bridge themselves. Fishermen were lending ropes. Women from the market were organising meals for the builders. The two villages had disagreed about ownership but agreed that a collapsed road helped neither side.

"Then they did not wait for the court?" asked the king.

"No," the courier answered.

The king might have punished them for acting without permission. He might have praised them. In the telling Bàbá Délé chose that evening, he did neither at first.

He walked alone to the palace gate and listened to the rain.

Kúnlé leaned forward.

"You are making him unusually thoughtful."

"Perhaps," said Bàbá Délé. "I told you this was a story I am shaping, not a word-for-word record. Another teller might give the king a fiercer voice. Another might dwell on consequences that my version has only begun to show."

Àdìkẹ́ nodded. "That is why we ought to ask where the telling came from."

The old man continued.

By morning the king's anger had not vanished. It had become a question: could he bear to be powerful without making everybody afraid to disagree?

## Chapter 4 — Three rivers, many voices

A girl in the audience raised her hand.

"Where are Ọya, Ọ̀ṣun and Ọ̀bà in this story?"

Bàbá Délé smiled. "In other tellings, their names belong to stories that connect to Ṣàngó. But they are not scenery behind one man's throne."

He invited the children to name what they had heard.

One had heard Ọya described with wind and change. Another associated Ọ̀ṣun with water, beauty and powerful care. A third knew of Ọ̀bà through a story about loyalty and loss. Their versions did not match precisely. Some were borrowed from books; some came from relatives; some had travelled across oceans and returned with altered names.

Kúnlé's younger cousin announced, "Then somebody must be wrong."

"Perhaps somebody has mixed up a detail," Àdìkẹ́ replied. "But a sacred tradition is not a puzzle where we put three names on one line and declare the answer finished."

Rain hammered the roof. A woman at the far side of the veranda explained that she belonged to a family for whom the names were spoken reverently, not as characters in a game. She did not offer details of practice, and nobody pressed her.

Bàbá Délé thanked her. Then he returned to his imagined king.

The palace messengers reported that both villages had brought timber to rebuild the bridge. One had brought wood. The other had brought rope. Neither contribution could stand alone.

The king looked at the materials laid before him.

"Which of the two is stronger?" he asked the courier.

"The bridge will tell us," the young man said, "when both have done their work."

This time the king did not shout.

## Chapter 5 — The axe with two faces

The rain softened. Across the square the custodian permitted the group to look more closely at the double-axe emblem, without handling it.

Kúnlé studied the carving. "It has two sides. Just like the bridge story."

"That is the meaning you found," said Àdìkẹ́. "Do not announce it as the only sacred meaning."

He looked again. The surface held marks left by the maker's tools. Another visitor pointed out that some carved staffs show figures bearing the emblem. The figure was not necessarily a portrait of Ṣàngó himself.

Bàbá Délé had finished his court story, but the children demanded an ending.

"What happened to the king?"

"Tell us whether he punished the officials."

"Did lightning strike the palace?"

The old man looked towards the clouds. "Different tellings can take a life in different directions. Some emphasise royal pride and an ending marked by calamity. Some speak from living devotion, where Ṣàngó cannot be contained in a tale about a fallen ruler. I will not pretend to settle those tellings with one invented lightning strike."

Kúnlé was disappointed.

He had wanted the storm to reveal a secret. A spectacular ending would have been easier to remember than a careful distinction.

Then Àdìkẹ́ asked, "What if the ending is not about whether lightning struck, but about what people do when they hear thunder?"

Kúnlé thought of the market vendors, the children, the music and the family who had spoken with reverence. The same sound had reached every ear. It had not arrived with a written explanation.

He began to understand why the answer he wanted might be smaller than the question.

## Chapter 6 — The story that stayed after rain

By sunset the clouds had broken.

The storytelling square glittered with puddles. Children splashed in the shallowest ones until they were called away. The drummer tested the skin of his instrument and began a rhythm that rose and fell against the smell of wet earth.

Kúnlé found Bàbá Délé folding his mat.

"I still believe thunder has a natural explanation," he said.

"Good," the old man replied.

"But I also see that the name Ṣàngó carries more than a weather report."

"Good again."

"Then why did you not just tell us what Ṣàngó really did?"

Bàbá Délé closed the mat and looked at the people leaving the square.

"Because the people who carry sacred knowledge deserve to speak for themselves. Because old royal accounts are not all identical. Because I can invent a court story without pretending I discovered the private words of a king who lived generations before me."

Kúnlé considered the bridge from the imagined tale. The courier had noticed what the powerful people failed to see. The villagers had solved a problem that pride had made difficult. He would remember that, even though no one had sworn those scenes had happened.

Àdìkẹ́ joined them. "Tomorrow," she said, "we can visit the library and look at objects made in different places. Later we might ask a custodian to share a version they want recorded. Not tonight."

The old man nodded.

Across the square a child shouted, "Thunder!"

But the sky was clear. He had only heard a cart rolling over wooden planks.

Everyone laughed, and the child laughed loudest.

Kúnlé wondered what future storytellers might do with that moment. Perhaps they would tell of a sky that spoke twice, or an axe with two faces, or a visitor who arrived demanding one answer and left carrying several questions.

He looked once more at the carved emblem before the custodian covered it.

Whatever else it represented, it had gathered people into a conversation larger than the storm.

The drums went on.
