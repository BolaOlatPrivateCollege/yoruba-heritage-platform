# Ìjàpá and the Sky King's Invitation

*An original imaginative folktale inspired by Yorùbá Ìjàpá trickster storytelling and the widespread motif of a feast in the sky. The particular invitation, characters' dialogue, contest and ending are literary inventions, not a transcription of an inherited version. The Sky King is an invented folktale character, not a representation of Olódùmarè.*

## Chapter 1 — A letter with wings

One morning, a pigeon landed in the marketplace carrying a leaf tied with golden thread. She cleared her throat three times, which was two times more than Ìjàpá considered necessary.

"An invitation from the Sky King!" she announced.

Every bird in the marketplace stopped talking.

The king, she explained, was holding a feast above the clouds. Each invited guest was to bring one good story and something to share. There would be music, dancing and enough food for everyone.

Ìjàpá, the tortoise, was particularly interested in the last part.

"How generous of His Majesty to remember me," he said.

The pigeon examined her leaf.

"Your name is not here."

"Naturally. A person of my importance is usually invited separately."

"Naturally," said the pigeon, folding the leaf.

Ìjàpá hurried home to his wife, Yanníbó, who was sorting beans. He announced that he would soon dine in the sky.

"Have you grown wings?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"Have you received an invitation?"

"Not exactly."

"Then you have two problems."

Ìjàpá sat beside her and helped himself to a handful of roasted groundnuts.

"Three problems," he said. "I am hungry already."

Yanníbó reminded him that a feast was an occasion for sharing. She suggested that he ask the birds whether the king would welcome an additional guest, rather than inventing an invitation.

But Ìjàpá was already thinking about how he might arrive in the sky without revealing that nobody had asked him to come.

That evening, he practised bowing before his reflection in a pot of water.

"Your Majesty," he said, "I am a distinguished traveller from the earth."

His reflection looked unconvinced.

## Chapter 2 — A bargain made of feathers

The birds gathered beneath a broad tree to discuss their journey. Ìjàpá arrived carrying a basket covered with cloth.

"My friends," he said, "you have wings, but who among you has my experience of royal conversation?"

The parrot raised one foot. "I can repeat a royal conversation until everyone becomes tired of hearing it."

The guinea fowl laughed.

Ìjàpá offered to compose their greeting to the king. In exchange, he asked them to help him travel to the feast. The pigeon agreed to ask the king's messenger whether an extra guest could attend.

The messenger returned with permission, on the condition that Ìjàpá obey the same rules as the other guests.

The birds lent him feathers to make a pair of magnificent imaginary wings, which in the magic of this folktale carried him into the sky. They made him promise to return every feather when the journey was over.

Ìjàpá admired himself. He turned one way, then another.

"Do I look like a king?"

"You look like a tortoise who owes us feathers," replied the parrot.

Before leaving, Yanníbó handed him a small basket of roasted groundnuts to share at the feast.

"Remember whose feathers you are wearing."

"Of course," he answered.

"And remember whose food you are carrying."

"Of course."

"And remember that the sky is a very long way from home."

Ìjàpá promised to remember everything.

As soon as the company rose above the trees, however, he began imagining the enormous dishes awaiting him. He wondered whether the king might serve three meals before sunset. He wondered whether dessert would be counted as a separate meal.

By the time the clouds opened before them, he had forgotten to wonder what he might give in return.

## Chapter 3 — The guest called Everybody

The Sky King's courtyard glittered like morning dew. Musicians played beneath a roof of silver clouds. The guests placed their contributions on a long table, and the king welcomed them.

"Tonight," he said, "let every good thing belong to everybody."

Ìjàpá's ears seemed to grow larger.

"Everybody," he whispered.

He approached the royal steward.

"In my village, guests sometimes choose special names for a celebration. I have chosen mine. Please call me Everybody."

The steward smiled. "An unusual name."

"An unusual guest," Ìjàpá replied.

When the first dish arrived, the steward announced that it was for everybody.

Ìjàpá stepped forward.

"That is me."

He took the largest portion. When a second dish arrived, he repeated the trick. By the third dish, the parrot was staring at him with an expression that would have frightened a less shameless animal.

"You know what the king meant," she whispered.

"Names are important," Ìjàpá replied through a mouthful of food.

The guinea fowl protested, but Ìjàpá pointed to the steward.

"He called my name. Would you have me disobey a royal official?"

Meanwhile, his basket of groundnuts remained unopened beneath his seat.

The pigeon went to the king and quietly explained what was happening. The king listened without interrupting.

Then he asked the musicians to stop playing.

"Everybody," he called.

Ìjàpá stood, smiling.

"Yes, Your Majesty?"

"Excellent," said the king. "Come and help us settle a small matter."

Ìjàpá bowed so deeply that his borrowed feathers brushed the floor. He expected a reward for his cleverness.

The birds exchanged glances. The parrot covered her beak with one wing.

## Chapter 4 — A very expensive name

The king asked the steward to bring the account of the feast.

"The guests have eaten," he said, "and it is time to thank those who prepared their meal."

A cook stepped forward with a list of ingredients. Another described the labour of arranging the tables. The musicians explained how long they had practised.

"Everybody has enjoyed the feast," said the king. "Everybody must therefore help with the work of clearing it away."

Ìjàpá's smile began to disappear.

"Your Majesty, I believe there has been a misunderstanding."

"A misunderstanding? Surely Everybody is your name."

The parrot made a sound that she immediately pretended was a cough.

The king continued. "Everybody must thank the cooks. Everybody must help the steward. And Everybody must ensure the other guests receive their fair share."

Ìjàpá looked at the enormous courtyard. He looked at his small legs. He looked at the birds, who were making no attempt to save him from his own invention.

"Perhaps," he suggested, "we could reconsider the meaning of everybody."

"A splendid suggestion," replied the king. "What does it mean?"

Ìjàpá inspected the floor.

"All the guests together."

"And when the food was announced?"

"All the guests together."

"And when you took it?"

Ìjàpá swallowed.

"I behaved as though it meant only me."

The king did not shout. He asked the steward to bring fresh portions for the guests who had been deprived of theirs. Then he told Ìjàpá that an apology ought to be followed by an honest effort to repair the harm.

Ìjàpá untied his basket of groundnuts.

They suddenly looked like the finest gift he had ever brought anywhere.

## Chapter 5 — The story that was not his

After the meal, the guests took turns telling their stories. The pigeon described a journey through a difficult season. The guinea fowl told a funny tale about a drum that nobody could play quietly.

When Ìjàpá's turn arrived, he stood before the king and announced that he would tell the greatest story in the world.

He began describing a magnificent bird who had flown through a storm to deliver an important message.

The pigeon tilted her head. It was her own adventure, which she had shared with him on the journey.

"Who told you that story?" the king asked.

Ìjàpá hesitated.

"A very distinguished source."

The pigeon raised her wing.

"The distinguished source is me."

The courtyard erupted in laughter.

Ìjàpá wanted the cloud beneath him to swallow him. He had borrowed feathers, borrowed a story and almost borrowed an entire feast without intending to return any of it.

"May I begin again?" he asked.

The king nodded.

This time Ìjàpá told the story of a tortoise who had named himself Everybody because he wanted to eat alone. He described how the name had brought him more responsibilities than he had expected. He even imitated the steward's astonished face.

The guests laughed so loudly that the musicians had to wait before beginning their next song.

When the laughter faded, Ìjàpá thanked the pigeon for her story and the birds for their feathers. He shared the groundnuts Yanníbó had prepared and helped the steward with the remaining work.

The king declared that the tortoise had at last brought something of his own.

"What is it?" Ìjàpá asked.

"A story you were willing to tell truthfully."

## Chapter 6 — The feast at home

At dawn, the birds prepared to return to earth. Ìjàpá was relieved when they agreed to carry him home in the magical manner of the tale, provided that he kept his promise about their feathers.

He returned every borrowed feather. The parrot counted hers twice.

Yanníbó was waiting beside the house.

"How was the feast?"

"Excellent," Ìjàpá replied.

"Did you share the groundnuts?"

"Eventually."

"Did you remember your promises?"

"Eventually."

"Did everybody enjoy the meal?"

Ìjàpá sighed.

"Please do not call me that."

Yanníbó burst out laughing.

That evening, they invited their neighbours to a small meal. Ìjàpá helped prepare it and made sure each visitor received a portion before he served himself.

When the children asked for a story, he described his visit to the Sky King. He admitted that the birds had helped him travel and that his clever name had almost ruined the celebration.

A child asked whether he would ever try such a trick again.

"Certainly not," Ìjàpá answered.

Yanníbó looked at him.

"Not that particular trick," he corrected himself.

Everyone laughed.

The pigeon, who had come for the meal, asked whether she might tell her own version of the adventure. Ìjàpá agreed, although he suspected that her account would contain rather more detail about his embarrassment.

When she finished, the children asked for both stories to be told again.

Ìjàpá discovered that sharing a tale did not make him lose it. Sharing a meal did not make him lose his friends. And returning what he had borrowed was the surest way to be welcomed the next time an invitation arrived.

From then on, whenever a host announced that food was for everybody, Ìjàpá waited patiently.

Usually.

And whenever he did not, Yanníbó only had to say one word.
