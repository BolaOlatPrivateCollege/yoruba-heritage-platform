# Lísàbí and the Farmers' Promise

*Original dramatic fiction inspired by Ẹ̀gbá oral traditions about Lísàbí Agbòngbò-Àkàlà and resistance to oppressive tribute. The dialogue, meetings and characters of Yẹ́mí and Bàbá Láyí are invented. This is not an authenticated recording of an Ẹ̀gbá elder, and accounts differ on political circumstances, methods and chronology.*

## Chapter 1 — The tribute basket

The first thing Yẹ́mí noticed was not the stranger's spear. It was his silence.

He sat beneath the market tree with two empty baskets at his feet, watching women count what remained of their crops after the tribute collectors had gone. His clothes were patched and his sandals badly worn. He looked less like somebody waiting for a fight than somebody listening for a particular word.

"How much did they take?" he asked.

"Enough," said the pepper seller.

"Enough for whom?"

Nobody answered.

Yẹ́mí was seventeen and had spent most of her life believing tribute was as unavoidable as the rainy season. Every household gave a portion. Every season brought another demand. The amount changed, but nobody could say who was entitled to question it.

At her father's farm the yam heaps were smaller than the year before. Her younger brother had stopped asking for a second bowl. The collectors' sacks grew heavier.

The stranger introduced himself as Lísàbí.

He made no grand announcement. He asked Yẹ́mí's father how many families had harvested less than they planted, and how often the collectors returned.

"That is not an interesting question," Bàbá Láyí said.

"It is," Lísàbí replied, "if every family thinks it suffers alone."

That evening Yẹ́mí found a small mark scratched beside the market tree: three lines joined at their ends, like neighbours holding hands.

She did not know who had made it.

She would see it again.

## Chapter 2 — The gathering that was not a gathering

A month later, everyone seemed to be talking about farms.

They discussed seedlings at the stream, hoe handles beneath the shade, market prices beside the pottery stalls. Men and women who had never shared much more than a greeting began comparing the true cost of a harvest.

Bàbá Láyí called it a change in the weather.

Yẹ́mí called it strange.

Her friend Dàpọ̀ whispered that the talks were organised. "Lísàbí says nobody can know how much strength a village has while all its households are shut behind separate doors."

"Who told you that?"

"Someone who heard it from someone who heard it from him."

Yẹ́mí laughed. "Then it must be true."

But she was curious enough to follow Dàpọ̀ one evening. They went to a clearing where farmers sat in a circle with broken hoe handles laid across their knees. The handles were not weapons. Each was evidence of work done with hands that ached.

Lísàbí stood in the middle.

"If we meet to boast," he said, "we will go home with nothing. If we meet to listen, we might learn why none of us has enough."

He asked the oldest farmer to speak first. The man described land left untilled after years of payments. A woman spoke of crops moved secretly to keep her children fed. A younger man confessed that he had blamed his neighbour for shortages that came from the demands placed on both families.

When it was Yẹ́mí's turn, she nearly refused.

Then she said, "My brother has forgotten what it feels like to eat until he is full."

No one laughed.

Afterward Lísàbí bent beside the fire and drew three lines in the dirt, joining their ends.

"That is the mark," Yẹ́mí whispered.

"Whose mark?" he asked.

"Yours."

He shook his head. "A mark belongs to the people who remember what it asks of them."

## Chapter 3 — A secret too large to hide

The collectors returned under a sky the colour of dust.

Their leader carried a carved staff and spoke as though the market already belonged to him.

"Where is the tribute?"

Bàbá Láyí looked at the baskets stacked outside his house. For the first time in his life, he did not step aside immediately.

"We must speak about how much is taken," he said.

The collector laughed. "You must speak? Since when?"

The village square filled with neighbours. The pepper seller stood beside Bàbá Láyí. The potter closed his stall and joined them. Yẹ́mí watched the collector's eyes move from face to face.

Something had changed. Nobody was willing to look away.

The collector demanded the names of the people who had organised them.

"All of us?" said the potter.

It was a reckless answer. The collector raised his staff. Two guards moved towards the crowd.

Lísàbí stepped forward, not with a sword but with an empty basket.

"You cannot measure the harvest of a whole region by frightening one household at a time," he said.

"Who made you their voice?"

"They are standing here. You can ask them yourself."

For a moment Yẹ́mí thought the guards would strike him. The air was so still that she could hear the dry grass bending beside the path.

Instead the collector ordered his men to take whatever they could carry. This time the farmers did not surrender their baskets. They stood together, and the men withdrew to report the resistance.

Nobody celebrated.

Lísàbí told them the truth: the demand would return, and perhaps with greater force.

Yẹ́mí's stomach turned. "Then why did we do this?"

"Because now they have seen that a village is not only a collection of houses."

## Chapter 4 — When every path had ears

After the confrontation, fear changed shape.

Before, people had feared the collector at their door. Now they feared the listener at the next table, the traveller who asked too many questions, and the friend who might speak carelessly after drinking palm wine.

Rumours grew. One said a huge army was coming. Another claimed Lísàbí had promised that no blade could touch anybody who followed him. A third said the farmers had discovered charms that made their bodies invisible.

Yẹ́mí asked him whether the stories were true.

"I know what I have heard," he replied. "I do not know who started them."

"Would it not help if the collectors believed we could vanish?"

"It might help for one night. Tomorrow the people who believed it might take a danger they cannot survive."

They argued about the road ahead. Some wanted immediate confrontation. Others pleaded for another negotiation. Families in nearby communities had heard different versions of their resistance, and not every household wanted the same outcome.

Bàbá Láyí called for patience. Dàpọ̀ called for action. Yẹ́mí was tired of being told that brave people never disagreed.

Then a message arrived from a village beyond the river.

Its farmers had refused to send their tribute. They asked whether anybody would stand beside them if the demand returned.

Lísàbí held the letter by the fire for a long time.

"If we answer yes," he said, "it will no longer be possible to pretend that this gathering is only about repairing hoes."

Yẹ́mí looked around the circle. They were frightened. They were divided. They still needed one another.

"Then let the answer be one we can keep," she said.

## Chapter 5 — The night of many footsteps

The night that followed would grow in the telling. By the time the story reached distant markets, people would speak of astonishing escapes and daring plans.

Yẹ́mí remembered it first by its sounds.

A door creaking open. Sandals touching damp earth. The distant thump of someone carrying a water pot. The low call of a night bird and then another call answering from farther away.

Every pathway seemed full of people who had made a difficult choice.

The old potter carried bandages. The pepper seller led a group towards shelter for families who did not want to be caught in fighting. Dàpọ̀ waited for the signal that would tell them where the collectors had gathered.

Lísàbí was not at the front of every group. He could not be in ten places at once. That, Yẹ́mí realised, was why they had needed a movement rather than a single fearless hero.

When an alarm sounded, everyone expected a collapse into confusion. Instead Yẹ́mí saw her father turn and lead frightened children to safety. She saw neighbours share water. She saw a man who had argued against the plan come back to help someone who had supported it.

The night did not turn into a clean song of glory. There were injuries, panic and moments when nobody knew what would happen next. But by dawn the old demand had been challenged in a way that could not be ignored.

Someone began shouting Lísàbí's name.

He raised a hand and asked them to stop.

"Before you sing about me," he said, "count who is missing."

## Chapter 6 — Whose victory was it?

Years later, Yẹ́mí returned to the market tree with her own daughter.

A new generation sat beneath the branches, where storytellers spoke about Lísàbí Agbòngbò-Àkàlà. Some described a master organiser who brought the farmers together. Others placed him at the centre of astonishing acts of strength. A few preferred to tell of the old burden of tribute and the long work by which communities sought freedom.

Yẹ́mí's daughter listened carefully.

"Was he the strongest man who ever lived?"

"I cannot measure that," Yẹ́mí answered.

"Did he really disappear when enemies chased him?"

"That is a version people tell."

"Did you see him?"

"I saw the man who asked our village to stand together," Yẹ́mí said. "The one in the songs is bigger than any man I knew. If you want the stories from other towns, ask the people who remember them."

Her daughter frowned. "Then what do we remember?"

Yẹ́mí pointed to the market. The pepper seller's grandchildren traded beside the potter's. Neighbours argued over prices. Children chased a runaway chicken between the stalls. Life had returned to its busy, ordinary shape.

"We remember that fear can make every house feel like an island," she said. "We remember that joining hands is not the same as agreeing about everything."

Above them the old mark had nearly disappeared from the tree: three lines, once clear, now worn by rain and time.

Her daughter traced it with a fingertip.

"And what did it ask of them?"

Yẹ́mí thought of empty baskets, night footsteps and the long silence before people dared to speak.

"Not to leave each other alone."

The girl considered this. Then she drew the three lines in the dust so the younger children could see them.

By sunset a storyteller would arrive and tell another version, perhaps one filled with astonishing battles, secret powers and a hero whose name had grown larger than a single life. People would gather to hear it. Some would recognise details, some would object, and some would add what their own families had preserved.

The market tree would listen to them all.
